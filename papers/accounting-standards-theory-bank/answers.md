# INTRODUCTION, STANDARD-SETTING AND APPLICABILITY – 2 MARKS

## Q1. Define an accounting standard. (Q.P. 54323) (Model Nov 2026)
An **accounting standard** is a written statement of rules for recognising, measuring, presenting and disclosing transactions in the financial statements. In India the Accounting Standards Board prepares them and the Council of the ICAI issues them. A standard cannot override the law.

## Q2. State any benefits of accounting standards. (Q.P. 54323)
Accounting standards are issued so that enterprises follow one accepted practice. Their benefits are:
1. **Standardisation:** similar transactions are recorded in one way.
2. **Disclosure:** the statements give the extra information users need.
3. **Comparability:** results of different enterprises, and of different years, can be compared.

## Q3. State any two objectives of setting accounting standards. (Model Nov 2026)
Accounting standards are set so that enterprises follow one accepted practice. Two objectives are:
1. **Uniformity:** similar transactions are recorded in one way.
2. **Comparability:** results of different enterprises, and of different years, can be compared.

## Q4. What is the Accounting Standards Board (ASB)?
The **Accounting Standards Board** is the body constituted by the ICAI in 1977 to prepare accounting standards. It identifies the areas that need a standard, prepares the drafts and exposure drafts, and sends the final standard to the Council of the ICAI for issue.

## Q5. What is the National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA)?
The **National Financial Reporting Authority** is the authority set up under the Companies Act, 2013. It recommends accounting standards and auditing standards for companies, and it monitors and enforces compliance with them. It can also look into the professional misconduct of auditors.

## Q6. What is an exposure draft?
An **exposure draft** is the draft of a proposed accounting standard issued for public comments. The ASB studies the comments received and modifies the draft before the standard is finally issued.

## Q7. What are Ind AS?
**Ind AS** (Indian Accounting Standards) are accounting standards converged with the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). They are notified by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs and apply, in phases, to listed and large companies. Other companies and non-company entities follow the Accounting Standards (AS).

## Q8. Who are MSMEs under the ICAI classification of non-company entities?
Level II, Level III and Level IV non-company entities are medium, small and micro entities, together called **MSMEs**. They are allowed certain relaxations, while Level I entities comply with every Accounting Standard in full.

## Q1. Explain the standard-setting process. (Q.P. 54323)
The ICAI constituted the Accounting Standards Board in 1977 to prepare standards. The standard-setting process is:
1. The area that needs a standard is identified.
2. A study group is formed and it prepares a draft.
3. The draft is circulated and the views of different bodies are taken.
4. An exposure draft is finalised and issued for public comments.
5. The draft is modified in the light of those comments.
6. The Council of the ICAI issues the Accounting Standard. For companies, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs notifies it.

A standard does not override the law. Where the law requires a different treatment, the law is followed and the departure is disclosed.

## Q2. Explain the standard-setting process in India by the Accounting Standards Board (ASB) and the National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA). (Model Nov 2026)

4. An exposure draft is finalised and issued for public comments.
5. The draft is modified in the light of those comments.

The National Financial Reporting Authority recommends accounting standards and auditing standards for companies, and it monitors and enforces compliance with them. It can also look into the professional misconduct of auditors. A standard does not override the law. Where the law requires a different treatment, the law is followed and the departure is disclosed.

## Q3. Explain the objectives and benefits of accounting standards.
Accounting standards lay down one accepted way of recognising, measuring, presenting and disclosing transactions, so that financial statements are reliable and can be compared.
1. **Uniformity:** similar transactions are recorded in one way by all enterprises.
2. **Comparability:** results of different enterprises, and of different years, can be compared.
3. **Disclosure:** the statements give the extra information users need.
4. **Fewer alternatives:** the choice between different accounting treatments is narrowed, which reduces manipulation of profits.
5. **Reliability:** users, such as investors, lenders and the government, can trust the statements.
6. **Help to auditors:** the auditor has a fixed benchmark against which to check the accounts.

## Q4. Explain the limitations of accounting standards.
Accounting standards bring uniformity, but they also have limits.
1. **Rigidity:** fixed rules may not suit every enterprise or every situation.
2. **Cannot override the law:** where a statute requires a different treatment, the law is followed.
3. **Choice still remains:** some standards allow more than one method, so full uniformity is not reached.
4. **Cost of compliance:** small enterprises find it costly to follow detailed standards, which is why relaxations are given.

# INTRODUCTION, STANDARD-SETTING AND APPLICABILITY – 10 MARKS

## Q1. Explain the criteria for classification of non-company entities as decided by the ICAI. (Q.P. 54323)
The Council of the ICAI, for accounting periods beginning on or after 1 April 2020, classifies non-company entities into four levels. Level I entities are large and comply with every Accounting Standard in full. Level II, Level III and Level IV are medium, small and micro entities. Together they are called MSMEs, and they are allowed certain relaxations.

### Level I
An entity is Level I if it falls in any one of these:
1. Its securities are listed, or are in the process of listing, in India or outside India.
2. It is a bank, including a co-operative bank, a financial institution, or it carries on insurance business.
3. Its turnover, excluding other income, exceeds Rs. 250 crore in the immediately preceding accounting year.
4. Its borrowings, including public deposits, exceed Rs. 50 crore at any time during that year.
5. It is a holding or subsidiary entity of any of the above.

### Level II
An entity that is not Level I is Level II if:
1. Turnover, excluding other income, exceeds Rs. 50 crore but does not exceed Rs. 250 crore, or
2. Borrowings, including public deposits, exceed Rs. 10 crore but do not exceed Rs. 50 crore, or
3. It is a holding or subsidiary entity of any such entity.

### Level III
An entity that is not Level I or Level II is Level III if:
1. Turnover, excluding other income, exceeds Rs. 10 crore but does not exceed Rs. 50 crore, or
2. Borrowings, including public deposits, exceed Rs. 2 crore but do not exceed Rs. 10 crore, or
3. It is a holding or subsidiary entity of any such entity.

### Level IV
Every non-company entity not covered above is a Level IV entity. An MSME that uses the relaxations discloses, in a note, that it is an MSME and states its level. This classification does not apply to a non-company entity that is required to follow Ind AS.

## Q2. What are accounting standards? Explain their objectives and the standard-setting process in India.
An **accounting standard** is a written statement of rules for recognising, measuring, presenting and disclosing transactions in the financial statements. In India the Accounting Standards Board (ASB), constituted by the ICAI in 1977, prepares them and the Council of the ICAI issues them.

### Objectives
1. **Uniformity:** similar transactions are recorded in one way.
2. **Comparability:** results of different enterprises, and of different years, can be compared.
3. **Disclosure:** the statements give the extra information users need.
4. **Fewer alternatives:** the choice of treatments is narrowed, which reduces manipulation.
5. **Reliability:** investors, lenders and the government can trust the statements.

### Standard-setting process
6. **Identification:** the area that needs a standard is identified.
7. **Draft:** a study group is formed and it prepares a draft, which is circulated for the views of different bodies.
8. **Exposure draft:** an exposure draft is finalised and issued for public comments.
9. **Modification:** the draft is modified in the light of those comments.
10. **Issue:** the Council of the ICAI issues the standard; for companies, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs notifies it, and the NFRA monitors and enforces compliance.

A standard does not override the law. Where the law requires a different treatment, the law is followed and the departure is disclosed.

# AS 7: CONSTRUCTION CONTRACTS – 2 MARKS

## Q1. What is a construction contract? (Q.P. 54323)
A **construction contract**, as per AS 7, is a contract specifically negotiated for the construction of an asset or a combination of assets that are closely interrelated or interdependent in terms of their design, technology and function or their ultimate purpose or use. Examples are contracts for a bridge, building, dam, road, ship or refinery.

## Q2. What is a fixed price contract?
A **fixed price contract** is a construction contract in which the contractor agrees to a fixed contract price, or a fixed rate per unit of output, which in some cases is subject to cost escalation clauses. It is common in public tenders for roads, flyovers and office buildings.

## Q3. What is a cost-plus contract?
A **cost-plus contract** is a construction contract in which the contractor is reimbursed for allowable or otherwise defined costs, plus a percentage of these costs or a fixed fee. It is used when the costs or the time of completion cannot be measured with certainty.

## Q4. What are retentions, progress billings and advances?
1. **Retentions:** amounts of progress billings that are not paid until the conditions in the contract are satisfied or defects are rectified.
2. **Progress billings:** amounts billed for work performed on a contract, whether or not the customer has paid them.
3. **Advances:** amounts received by the contractor before the related work is performed.

## Q5. What does contract revenue comprise?
**Contract revenue** comprises the initial amount of revenue agreed in the contract, and variations in contract work, claims and incentive payments, to the extent that it is probable that they will result in revenue and they can be measured reliably. Penalties for late completion reduce it.

## Q6. What is the percentage of completion method?
The **percentage of completion method** (stage of completion method) recognises contract revenue and contract costs each year in proportion to the stage of completion of the work. It follows the accrual and matching concepts, and is used only when the final outcome of the contract can be estimated reliably.

## Q7. How is an expected loss on a construction contract treated?
When it is probable that total contract costs will exceed total contract revenue, the **expected loss** is recognised as an expense immediately. This follows the prudence concept, whatever the stage of completion of the work.

## Q8. What are costs relating to future activity?
**Costs relating to future activity** are contract costs incurred for work not yet performed, such as materials bought but not yet used. They are recognised as an asset, usually called contract work in progress, provided it is probable that they will be recovered.

# AS 7: CONSTRUCTION CONTRACTS – 5 MARKS

## Q1. Explain the significance and scope of AS 7.
Construction contracts usually take more than one accounting year to complete, so revenue and costs must be allocated to the periods in which the work is performed. AS 7 prescribes this accounting in the financial statements of contractors.
1. **Two parties:** there is a contractor, who builds, and a contractee or customer, who wants the construction. An asset built by an enterprise for its own use is not covered; it falls under AS 10.
2. **Single asset:** a contract for one asset, such as a bridge, building, dam, pipeline, road, ship or tunnel.
3. **Combination of assets:** a contract for assets closely interrelated in design, technology, function or use, such as a refinery or complex plant.
4. **Related services:** contracts for services directly related to construction, such as those of project managers and architects.
5. **Destruction and restoration:** contracts for destruction or restoration of assets, and restoration of the environment after demolition.

## Q2. Explain the components of contract revenue.
Contract revenue comprises the initial amount agreed in the contract and the variations, claims and incentive payments, to the extent that they will probably result in revenue and can be measured reliably.
1. **Agreed price:** the fixed price, or the cost-plus price, in the contract.
2. **Cost escalation:** agreed increases in the price for higher costs.
3. **Variations:** changes in the scope of work asked for by the customer.
4. **Claims:** amounts the contractor seeks for costs not included in the contract price, included only when the customer will probably accept them.
5. **Incentive payments:** extra amounts, usually for early completion, included only when the performance standards will probably be met.
6. **Penalties:** amounts deducted, usually for late completion.

## Q3. Explain the contract costs as per AS 7.
Contract costs comprise costs that relate directly to the specific contract, costs attributable to contract activity in general that can be allocated to the contract, and other costs specifically chargeable to the customer under the contract.
### Direct costs
1. **Site costs:** site labour, including site supervision, and materials used in construction.
2. **Plant costs:** depreciation of plant used on the contract, hire of plant, and moving plant and materials to and from the site.
3. **Other direct costs:** design and technical assistance directly related to the contract, estimated rectification and guarantee work, and claims from third parties.
### Allocable costs
4. **General costs:** insurance, design and technical assistance not related to one contract, and construction overheads, allocated on normal levels of activity. Borrowing costs may be included as per AS 16.
### Excluded costs
5. **Not contract costs:** general administration and research and development costs not reimbursable under the contract, selling costs, and depreciation of idle plant not used on a particular contract.

## Q4. When can the outcome of a construction contract be estimated reliably?
The percentage of completion method is used only when the outcome of the contract can be estimated reliably. AS 7 gives separate conditions for the two types of contract.
### Fixed price contract
1. Total contract revenue can be measured reliably.
2. It is probable that the economic benefits of the contract will flow to the enterprise.
3. Both the contract costs to complete the contract and the stage of completion at the reporting date can be measured reliably.
4. The contract costs can be clearly identified and measured reliably, so that actual costs can be compared with earlier estimates.
### Cost-plus contract
5. It is probable that the economic benefits of the contract will flow to the enterprise.
6. The contract costs, whether or not specifically reimbursable, can be clearly identified and measured reliably.

If the outcome cannot be estimated reliably, revenue is recognised only to the extent of contract costs incurred that will probably be recovered, and contract costs are expensed as incurred.

## Q5. Explain the methods of determining the stage of completion.
The stage of completion is determined by the method that measures reliably the work performed. Progress payments and advances received may not reflect the work performed.
1. **Proportion of costs incurred:** costs incurred to date ÷ estimated total contract costs × 100. Useful where costs are closely monitored, as in private contracts.
2. **Surveys of work performed:** a surveyor appointed for the project certifies the percentage of work completed. Common in government projects.
3. **Physical proportion completed:** the work done is measured physically, for example 300 sq. ft. of tiles laid out of 1,000 sq. ft. is 30% complete.

## Q6. Explain combining and segmenting of construction contracts.
AS 7 normally applies to each contract separately, but sometimes it is applied to parts of one contract, or to a group of contracts, to show the substance of the arrangement.
### Segmenting
When a contract covers a number of assets, each asset is a separate construction contract when:
1. separate proposals have been submitted for each asset,
2. each asset has been subject to separate negotiation, and the contractor and customer could accept or reject that part, and
3. the costs and revenues of each asset can be identified.
### Combining
A group of contracts, with one or several customers, is treated as a single construction contract when:
4. the group is negotiated as a single package,
5. the contracts are so closely interrelated that they are in effect part of a single project with an overall profit margin, and
6. the contracts are performed concurrently or in a continuous sequence.
### Additional asset
7. An additional asset at the customer's option is a separate contract when it differs significantly in design, technology or function, or its price is negotiated without regard to the original contract price.

## Q7. Distinguish between a fixed price contract and a cost-plus contract.
| Basis | Fixed price contract | Cost-plus contract |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Fixed sum or fixed rate per unit | Allowable costs plus a percentage or fixed fee |
| Risk of cost rise | Borne by the contractor, unless an escalation clause applies | Borne mainly by the customer |
| Used when | Costs can be budgeted, as in public tenders | Scope or cost is uncertain |
| Reliable outcome | Four conditions must be met | Two conditions must be met |
| Profit | Not assured | Assured margin on cost |

## Q8. Explain the disclosure requirements of AS 7.
AS 7 requires disclosure of how contract revenue is recognised and of the position of contracts in progress.
1. **Revenue:** the amount of contract revenue recognised in the period.
2. **Methods:** the methods used to determine contract revenue and the stage of completion of contracts in progress.
3. **Contracts in progress:** the aggregate costs incurred and recognised profits (less recognised losses) up to the reporting date.
4. **Advances and retentions:** the amount of advances received and the amount of retentions.
5. **Gross amounts:** the gross amount due from customers is shown as an asset, and the gross amount due to customers as a liability. It equals costs incurred + recognised profits − recognised losses − progress billings.

## Q9. It is argued that profit on construction contracts should not be recognised until the contract is completed. Would this improve the quality of financial reporting?
No. Construction contracts are usually long term: they are entered into in one accounting period, but the work flows into more than one accounting year.
1. **Distorted results:** if profit is not recognised over the construction period, the costs of the early years are recognised without any matching revenue. This shows losses in the early years followed by high profits later.
2. **Matching:** the treatment under AS 7 recognises revenue and the related costs in the same period.
3. **Prudence:** AS 7 also follows prudence, because any foreseeable loss is recognised immediately.

So AS 7 gives a fair representation of the financial substance of the contract, and waiting until completion would not improve reporting.

# AS 7: CONSTRUCTION CONTRACTS – 10 MARKS

## Q1. XYZ Construction Ltd. undertakes an industrial complex. Separate proposals were raised for each unit. Each unit was subject to separate negotiation, and the costs and revenues of each unit can be identified. Should each unit be treated as a separate construction contract under AS 7? (Model Nov 2026)
AS 7 says that where one contract covers a number of assets, the construction of each asset is a separate construction contract when three conditions are met:
1. separate proposals have been submitted for each asset,
2. each asset has been subject to separate negotiation, so that the contractor and the customer could accept or reject that part, and
3. the costs and revenues of each asset can be identified.

All three conditions are met for the units of the industrial complex. XYZ Construction Ltd. treats the construction of each unit as a **separate construction contract**.

## Q2. Explain Accounting Standard 7 on construction contracts.
AS 7 prescribes the accounting for construction contracts in the financial statements of contractors. A **construction contract** is a contract specifically negotiated for the construction of an asset or a combination of closely interrelated assets. Such contracts usually cover more than one accounting year, so the standard decides when contract revenue and contract costs are recognised in the statement of profit and loss.
### Scope and types
1. **Scope:** contracts for an asset or group of assets, related services of architects and project managers, and destruction or restoration of assets.
2. **Fixed price contract:** a fixed price or fixed rate per unit, sometimes with escalation.
3. **Cost-plus contract:** allowable costs plus a percentage or a fixed fee.
4. **Combining and segmenting:** separately proposed, negotiated and costed assets are separate contracts; a single package forming one project is one contract.
### Revenue and costs
5. **Contract revenue:** the agreed amount plus variations, claims and incentives that will probably result in revenue and can be measured reliably.
6. **Contract costs:** direct costs, allocable general costs and costs chargeable to the customer; selling, general administration, unreimbursed research and idle-plant depreciation are excluded.
### Recognition
7. **Percentage of completion:** when the outcome can be estimated reliably, revenue and costs are recognised by reference to the stage of completion, measured by costs incurred, surveys or physical proportion.
8. **Outcome not reliable:** revenue only to the extent of recoverable costs incurred; costs expensed as incurred.
9. **Expected loss:** recognised as an expense immediately. Changes in estimates are treated as changes in accounting estimate under AS 5, and an amount later found uncollectable is an expense, not a cut in revenue.
### Disclosure
10. **Disclosure:** contract revenue recognised, the methods used, costs and recognised profits to date, advances, retentions, and the gross amounts due from and to customers.

## Q3. Explain the recognition of contract revenue and expenses under AS 7.
Construction contracts are long term, so the final profit or loss is known only after some years. AS 7 recognises revenue every year in proportion to the progress of work, matched with the costs of that work, provided the outcome can be estimated reliably.
### Outcome can be estimated reliably
1. **Percentage of completion method:** revenue and costs are recognised by reference to the stage of completion at the reporting date.
2. **Fixed price conditions:** total revenue measurable, benefits probable, costs to complete and stage of completion measurable, and costs clearly identifiable.
3. **Cost-plus conditions:** benefits probable and costs clearly identifiable and measurable.
4. **Stage of completion:** by costs incurred to estimated total costs, by surveys of work performed, or by physical proportion of work done.
5. **Future activity:** costs for work not yet done are shown as an asset (contract work in progress) if they will probably be recovered.
### Outcome cannot be estimated reliably
6. **Revenue:** recognised only to the extent of contract costs incurred that will probably be recovered.
7. **Costs:** recognised as an expense in the period incurred. When the uncertainty ends, the percentage of completion method is applied.
### Special points
8. **Expected loss:** when total costs will probably exceed total revenue, the whole expected loss is expensed immediately.
9. **Uncollectable amounts:** an amount already recognised that becomes doubtful is shown as an expense, not as a reduction of revenue.
10. **Changes in estimates:** the method is applied cumulatively, and the effect of a change is treated as a change in accounting estimate under AS 5.

# AS 9: REVENUE RECOGNITION – 2 MARKS

## Q1. Define revenue. (Q.P. 54323)
**Revenue** is the gross inflow of cash, receivables or other consideration arising in the course of the ordinary activities of an enterprise, from the sale of goods, the rendering of services, and the use by others of enterprise resources yielding interest, royalties and dividends. Amounts collected on behalf of third parties, such as GST, are not revenue.

## Q2. State the timing of revenue recognition from the sale of goods under AS-9. (Model Nov 2026)
Revenue from the sale of goods is recognised when the seller has transferred the property in the goods for a price. That point usually coincides with the transfer of the significant risks and rewards of ownership. If the two do not coincide, revenue is recognised when the significant risks and rewards pass to the buyer.

## Q3. What is the completed service contract method?
The **completed service contract method** recognises revenue in the statement of profit and loss only when the rendering of services under a contract is completed or substantially completed. It is used when a single or critical act is essential to the service.

## Q4. What is the proportionate completion method?
The **proportionate completion method** recognises revenue in the statement of profit and loss proportionately with the degree of completion of services under a contract. It is used for ongoing service contracts with several stages.

## Q5. How are interest, royalties and dividends recognised under AS 9?
1. **Interest:** on a time proportion basis, taking into account the amount outstanding and the rate applicable.
2. **Royalties:** on an accrual basis, in accordance with the terms of the relevant agreement.
3. **Dividends:** when the owner's right to receive payment is established.

## Q6. Name any four items excluded from AS 9.
1. Revenue from construction contracts (AS 7).
2. Revenue from hire-purchase and lease agreements (AS 19).
3. Revenue from government grants and similar subsidies (AS 12).
4. Revenue of insurance companies from insurance contracts.

## Q7. How is uncertainty about collection treated under AS 9?
If collection is not reasonably certain at the time of sale, **revenue recognition is postponed** until the uncertainty is resolved. If the uncertainty arises after revenue has been recognised, a separate provision or bad-debt expense is made; the revenue already recorded is not reversed.

# AS 9: REVENUE RECOGNITION – 5 MARKS

## Q1. Explain the objectives of AS 9.
AS 9 is mainly concerned with the timing of recognition of revenue in the statement of profit and loss. It follows the prudence principle: revenue is not recognised until it is measurable and reasonably certain.
1. **Standardise timing:** fix uniform criteria for when revenue is credited to profit and loss.
2. **Prevent early booking:** stop revenue being recognised before the risks and rewards pass.
3. **Handle uncertainty:** give rules for deferring revenue when collection is doubtful.
4. **Specify sources:** separate the rules for goods, services, interest, royalties and dividends.
5. **Mandate disclosure:** ensure that revenue postponed because of uncertainty is disclosed.

## Q2. Explain the scope of AS 9.
AS 9 deals with revenue arising in the course of the ordinary activities of an enterprise. Only ordinary revenue is covered.
### Covered
1. **Sale of goods:** transfer of ownership in goods, products or materials.
2. **Rendering of services:** performance of service contracts, over time or on completion.
3. **Use of enterprise resources:** interest, royalties (for patents, trademarks, copyrights and know-how) and dividends.
### Not covered
4. **Other standards:** construction contracts (AS 7), hire-purchase and lease agreements (AS 19), government grants (AS 12), and revenue of insurance companies from insurance contracts.
5. **Other gains:** gains on disposal of non-current assets, unrealised holding gains, natural growth of livestock or agricultural assets, and extraction of minerals.

## Q3. Explain the recognition of revenue from the sale of goods under AS 9.
Revenue from the sale of goods is recognised when the seller has transferred the property in the goods to the buyer for a consideration. This usually coincides with the transfer of the significant risks and rewards of ownership. Two conditions must be met:
1. **Transfer of risks and rewards:** the seller has transferred the significant risks and rewards of ownership and keeps no effective control or managerial involvement.
2. **Reasonable certainty:** there is no significant uncertainty about the amount of consideration or its ultimate collection.

Where transfer of property and transfer of risks and rewards do not coincide, revenue is recognised when the risks and rewards pass. This may happen because of:
3. **Delay in delivery:** a delay caused by either the buyer or the seller. If delivery is delayed at the buyer's request and the buyer takes title, revenue is recognised.
4. **Agreement:** an agreement between the parties fixing the time when risks and rewards pass, for example guaranteed sales recognised on the buyer's acceptance.

## Q4. Explain the recognition of revenue from the rendering of services.
Revenue from service transactions is recognised as the service is performed, by one of two methods. Performance is measured by the work completed or the acts performed.
1. **Proportionate completion method:** revenue is recognised proportionately with the degree of completion of services under the contract. It suits ongoing, multi-stage service contracts.
2. **Completed service contract method:** revenue is recognised only when the services are completed or substantially completed. It suits contracts where a single or critical act is essential.
3. **Uncertainty:** in both methods, revenue is recognised only when there is no significant uncertainty about measurement or collection.

## Q5. Explain the recognition of interest, royalties and dividends.
When others use the resources of an enterprise, the enterprise earns interest, royalties and dividends. They are recognised only when no significant uncertainty about measurement or collection exists.
1. **Interest:** recognised on a time proportion basis, taking the amount outstanding and the rate applicable. For example, interest on a deposit for January to March is recognised in March, even if it is received in June.
2. **Royalties:** recognised on an accrual basis in accordance with the agreement. If the royalty depends on the number of copies sold, it is recognised on that basis.
3. **Dividends:** recognised when the owner's right to receive payment is established, that is when the dividend is declared. A dividend merely proposed is not yet revenue.

## Q6. Explain the treatment of uncertainties in revenue recognition.
AS 9 recognises revenue only when it is measurable and its collection is reasonably certain. Uncertainty is treated in two ways, depending on when it arises.
1. **Uncertainty at the time of sale:** if collection cannot be reasonably assessed when the invoice is raised or the service is performed, revenue recognition is postponed until collection becomes reasonably certain.
2. **Subsequent uncertainty:** if collection becomes doubtful after revenue has been properly recognised, a bad-debt provision or expense is created. The revenue already recognised is not adjusted.
3. **Postponed revenue:** revenue postponed because of uncertainty is recognised in the period in which it becomes reasonably certain, and it is disclosed.

## Q7. What disclosures are required by AS 9?
AS 9 requires disclosures in the notes to accounts, as per Schedule III, so that users can see where revenue has been deferred.
1. **Accounting policy:** the circumstances in which revenue recognition has been postponed pending the resolution of significant uncertainties.
2. **Postponed revenue:** the amount and nature of items deferred, with the nature of the uncertainty.
3. **Service method:** the method used for service revenue, proportionate completion or completed service contract.
4. **Statutory collections:** turnover shown gross, with excise duty or GST shown as a deduction.

## Q8. Explain the importance of AS 9.
AS 9 is the basic standard of revenue accounting in India and applies to all commercial enterprises whatever their size.
1. **Financial transparency:** gives a true and fair view of top-line earnings without manipulation.
2. **Comparability:** performance can be compared across periods and with other enterprises.
3. **Fraud prevention:** stops aggressive revenue recognition and channel stuffing.
4. **Audit verification:** gives auditors a standard benchmark to check revenue.
5. **Investor protection:** protects the capital market from inflated earnings.

# AS 9: REVENUE RECOGNITION – 10 MARKS

## Q1. Explain Accounting Standard 9 in detail. (Model Nov 2026)
**Revenue** is the inflow of cash, receivables or other consideration arising in the course of the ordinary activities of an enterprise. AS 9 deals with when that revenue is recognised, and with the effect of uncertainty about the amount or its collection. It does not apply to construction contracts, hire-purchase and lease agreements, government grants, or the revenue of insurance companies.

Revenue covered by AS 9 arises from:
1. the sale of goods,
2. the rendering of services, and
3. the use by others of enterprise resources, which yields interest, royalties and dividends.

**Sale of goods.** Revenue is recognised when the seller has transferred the property in the goods to the buyer for a price. That normally coincides with the transfer of the significant risks and rewards of ownership. Where it does not, revenue waits until those risks and rewards have passed. A delay in delivery, whether the buyer's or the seller's, or an agreement fixing the time of transfer, can separate the two events.

**Rendering of services.** Revenue is recognised as the service is performed, by one of two methods:
1. **Completed-service method:** revenue is recognised only when the service is completed or substantially completed.
2. **Proportionate-completion method:** revenue is recognised in step with the degree of completion.

**Interest, royalties and dividends.**
1. **Interest** is recognised on a time basis, taking the amount outstanding and the rate.
2. **Royalties** are recognised on an accrual basis, as the agreement provides.
3. **Dividends** are recognised when the owner's right to receive payment is established.

Revenue is recognised only when it can be measured reliably and collection is reasonably certain. If collectability becomes uncertain after recognition, the amount already recorded is not reversed. A separate provision is made instead.

## Q2. What is revenue? Explain the objectives, scope, recognition rules and disclosures of AS 9.
**Revenue** is the gross inflow of cash, receivables or other consideration arising in the ordinary course of business from the sale of goods, the rendering of services and the use of enterprise resources. Amounts collected for third parties, such as GST, are excluded.
### Objectives and scope
1. **Objectives:** fix the timing of revenue recognition, prevent early booking, handle uncertainty, separate the sources of revenue, and require disclosure.
2. **Covered:** sale of goods, rendering of services, and interest, royalties and dividends.
3. **Not covered:** construction contracts, hire-purchase and leases, government grants, insurance contracts, and gains on disposal of non-current assets.
### Recognition
4. **Sale of goods:** when the significant risks and rewards of ownership pass to the buyer and there is no significant uncertainty about the amount or collection.
5. **Services:** by the proportionate completion method or the completed service contract method.
6. **Interest and royalties:** interest on a time proportion basis; royalties on an accrual basis as per the agreement.
7. **Dividends:** when the right to receive payment is established.
8. **Uncertainty:** if collection is uncertain at the time of sale, recognition is postponed; if it arises later, a provision is made and revenue is not reversed.
### Disclosure
9. **Postponed revenue:** the circumstances, amounts and nature of uncertainty where revenue recognition has been postponed.
10. **Other disclosures:** the method used for service revenue, and turnover shown gross with statutory collections deducted.

# AS 10: PROPERTY, PLANT AND EQUIPMENT – 2 MARKS

## Q1. Define property, plant and equipment as per AS 10. (Q.P. 54323)
**Property, plant and equipment**, as per AS 10 (Revised), are tangible items held for use in the production or supply of goods or services, for rental to others, or for administrative purposes. They are expected to be used during more than one period, that is for more than 12 months.

## Q2. What constitutes the cost of an item of Property, Plant, and Equipment under AS-10? (Model Nov 2026)
The cost of an item of property, plant and equipment, as per AS 10, is:
1. the purchase price, including import duties and non-refundable purchase taxes, after trade discounts and rebates,
2. costs directly attributable to bringing the asset to the location and condition needed for its intended use, and
3. the initial estimate of dismantling, removal and site-restoration costs.

## Q3. State the recognition criteria for property, plant and equipment.
The cost of an item is recognised as property, plant and equipment only if:
1. it is probable that future economic benefits from the item will flow to the enterprise, and
2. the cost of the item can be measured reliably.

## Q4. What is the revaluation model?
Under the **revaluation model**, an item of property, plant and equipment whose fair value can be measured reliably is carried at a revalued amount. The revalued amount is the fair value at the date of revaluation less any later accumulated depreciation and impairment losses.

## Q5. What is the cost model?
Under the **cost model**, an item of property, plant and equipment is carried at its cost less accumulated depreciation and any accumulated impairment losses.

## Q6. What is derecognition of property, plant and equipment?
**Derecognition** is the removal of an item from the books on its disposal, or when no future economic benefits are expected from its use or disposal. The gain or loss, being net disposal proceeds less carrying amount, goes to profit and loss and is not classified as revenue.

## Q7. What is a bearer plant?
A **bearer plant** is a plant used in the production or supply of agricultural produce. Plants grown to be harvested as produce, and annual crops such as wheat and maize, are not bearer plants. Bearer plants are covered by AS 10, while other biological assets are not.

## Q8. How are day-to-day servicing costs treated under AS 10?
**Day-to-day servicing costs**, such as labour, small parts and consumables used for repairs and maintenance, are not added to the carrying amount of the asset. They are recognised in the statement of profit and loss when incurred.

## Q9. What are depreciable amount, useful life and residual value?
1. **Depreciable amount:** the cost of an asset, or the amount substituted for cost, less its residual value.
2. **Useful life:** the period over which the asset is expected to be used by the enterprise, or the number of production units expected from it.
3. **Residual value:** the amount the enterprise expects to obtain for the asset at the end of its useful life, after deducting the expected costs of disposal.

# AS 10: PROPERTY, PLANT AND EQUIPMENT – 5 MARKS

## Q1. Explain the objective and scope of AS 10.
The main objective of AS 10 is to prescribe the accounting treatment for property, plant and equipment, so that users understand the investment an enterprise has made in these assets and the changes in it.
1. **Covered:** tangible assets held for production or supply of goods or services, for rental, or for administration, including assets for selling and distribution, finance, personnel, safety and environmental purposes.
2. **Bearer plants:** plants used to produce agricultural produce are covered.
3. **Not covered, biological assets:** living animals and plants related to agricultural activity, other than bearer plants.
4. **Not covered, wasting assets:** mineral rights, and expenditure on exploration and extraction of minerals, oil, natural gas and similar non-regenerative resources.

## Q2. Explain the elements of cost of property, plant and equipment.
AS 10 measures an item at cost on initial recognition. Cost covers the initial cost of acquiring or constructing the item and later costs of adding to or replacing it.
1. **Purchase price:** including import duties and non-refundable purchase taxes, less trade discounts and rebates.
2. **Directly attributable costs:** freight and handling, site preparation, installation and other costs that bring the asset to the location and condition needed for its intended use.
3. **Dismantling costs:** the initial estimate of dismantling and removing the item and restoring the site.
4. **Exchange for shares:** an asset acquired for shares is recorded at its fair value or the fair value of the shares issued, whichever is more clearly evident.
5. **Costs not included:** refundable taxes, inauguration costs, staff training, initial operating losses and day-to-day servicing are charged to profit and loss.

## Q3. Explain the measurement of property, plant and equipment after recognition.
After recognition, an enterprise chooses one of two models for each class of property, plant and equipment.
1. **Cost model:** the item is carried at cost less accumulated depreciation and accumulated impairment losses.
2. **Revaluation model:** the item is carried at a revalued amount, which is fair value at the date of revaluation less later accumulated depreciation and impairment losses. It is used only when fair value can be measured reliably.
3. **Revaluation increase:** credited to revaluation surplus, the difference between the revalued amount and the carrying amount.
4. **Revaluation decrease:** charged to profit and loss, except to the extent of an existing revaluation surplus on the same asset.
5. **Depreciation after revaluation:** the revalued amount is written off over the remaining useful life.

## Q4. Explain the provisions of AS 10 on depreciation.
**Depreciation** is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of an asset over its useful life. Depreciable amount is cost, or the amount substituted for cost, less residual value.
1. **Component approach:** each part of an item whose cost is significant in relation to the total cost is depreciated separately.
2. **Systematic basis:** the depreciable amount is allocated on a systematic basis over the useful life.
3. **Charge to profit and loss:** depreciation is recognised in profit and loss unless it is included in the carrying amount of another asset.
4. **Review:** residual value and useful life are reviewed at each balance sheet date.
5. **Method:** the method must reflect the pattern in which the asset's benefits are consumed, and is reviewed at least at each financial year end. Methods include straight-line, written-down value and units of production.

## Q5. Distinguish between the cost model and the revaluation model.
| Basis | Cost model | Revaluation model |
|---|---|---|
| Carrying amount | Cost less depreciation and impairment | Fair value at revaluation less later depreciation and impairment |
| Condition | No special condition | Fair value must be measurable reliably |
| Revaluation surplus | Does not arise | Increase is credited to revaluation surplus |
| Depreciation | On cost | On the revalued amount |
| Updating | Values not updated | Revalued regularly |

# AS 10: PROPERTY, PLANT AND EQUIPMENT – 10 MARKS

## Q1. Explain AS 10 on property, plant and equipment.
**Property, plant and equipment** are tangible items held for use in the production or supply of goods or services, for rental to others, or for administrative purposes, and expected to be used for more than one period. AS 10 prescribes their recognition, measurement, depreciation and derecognition.
### Scope and recognition
1. **Scope:** covers all such tangible assets and bearer plants; excludes other biological assets and wasting assets such as mineral rights.
2. **Recognition:** an item is recognised only if future economic benefits will probably flow to the enterprise and its cost can be measured reliably.
### Measurement at recognition
3. **Cost:** purchase price with non-refundable duties and taxes, less trade discounts; directly attributable costs; and the estimate of dismantling and site-restoration costs.
4. **Excluded costs:** refundable taxes, inauguration, staff training, initial operating losses and day-to-day servicing.
5. **Subsequent costs:** replacing a significant part is capitalised and the replaced part is derecognised; routine repairs are expensed.
### Measurement after recognition
6. **Cost model:** cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment losses.
7. **Revaluation model:** fair value less later depreciation and impairment; increases go to revaluation surplus, decreases to profit and loss.
### Depreciation
8. **Depreciation:** each significant part is depreciated separately over its useful life by a method that reflects the pattern of benefits; useful life, residual value and method are reviewed every year.
### Derecognition and disclosure
9. **Derecognition:** on disposal, or when no future benefits are expected; the gain or loss goes to profit and loss and is not revenue.
10. **Disclosure:** for each class, the measurement bases, depreciation methods, useful lives or rates, and a reconciliation of the carrying amount at the beginning and end of the period.

# AS 15: EMPLOYEE BENEFITS – 2 MARKS

## Q1. State the types of employee benefits. (Q.P. 54323)
AS 15 (Revised) divides employee benefits into four types:
1. **Short-term benefits**, such as wages, paid leave and bonuses payable within twelve months.
2. **Post-employment benefits**, such as gratuity and pension.
3. **Other long-term benefits**, such as long-service leave.
4. **Termination benefits**, payable when employment comes to an end.

## Q2. What is the objective of AS 15?
The **objective of AS 15** is to prescribe the accounting and disclosure for employee benefits. It requires an enterprise to recognise a liability when an employee has provided service in exchange for benefits to be paid in future, and an expense when the enterprise consumes the benefit of that service.

## Q3. What are short-term employee benefits?
**Short-term employee benefits** are benefits, other than termination benefits, that fall due wholly within twelve months after the end of the period in which the employees render the service. Examples are wages, salaries, paid annual leave, sick leave, bonuses and non-monetary benefits such as medical care and housing.

## Q4. What are post-employment benefits?
**Post-employment benefits** are benefits payable after the completion of employment. Examples are gratuity, pension, provident fund, post-employment life insurance and post-employment medical care.

## Q5. What are termination benefits?
**Termination benefits** are employee benefits payable because of the enterprise's decision to end an employee's employment before the normal retirement date, or the employee's decision to accept voluntary redundancy (voluntary retirement) in exchange for those benefits. VRS payments are an example.

## Q6. What is a defined contribution plan?
A **defined contribution plan** is a post-employment benefit plan under which the enterprise pays fixed contributions into a separate fund and has no obligation to pay more if the fund is short. The employee bears the risk. Provident fund is an example.

## Q7. What is a defined benefit plan?
A **defined benefit plan** is a post-employment benefit plan other than a defined contribution plan. The enterprise promises an agreed benefit, so the actuarial and investment risk falls on the enterprise. Gratuity and pension are examples.

## Q8. How are actuarial gains and losses treated under AS 15?
**Actuarial gains and losses** arise from changes in actuarial assumptions and from differences between assumptions and actual experience. Under AS 15 (Revised) they are recognised immediately in the statement of profit and loss as income or expense.

## Q9. How is the actual return on plan assets calculated?
**Actual return on plan assets** = Fair value of plan assets at the end of the year − Fair value at the beginning − Contributions received + Benefits paid. It includes interest, dividends and gains on the plan assets, less administration costs.

## Q10. What are other long-term employee benefits?
**Other long-term employee benefits** are benefits, other than post-employment and termination benefits, that do not fall due wholly within twelve months after the end of the period of service. Examples are long-service or sabbatical leave, long-term disability benefits, and bonuses or deferred compensation payable after twelve months.

# AS 15: EMPLOYEE BENEFITS – 5 MARKS

## Q1. Explain the types of employee benefits under AS 15.
AS 15 (Revised 2005) has four types of employee benefits. Each has different features, so the standard sets separate recognition, measurement and disclosure rules for each.
1. **Short-term employee benefits:** wages, salaries and social security contributions, paid annual leave and sick leave, profit-sharing and bonuses payable within twelve months of the end of the period, and non-monetary benefits such as medical care, housing, cars and free or subsidised goods for current employees.
2. **Post-employment benefits:** gratuity, pension, other retirement benefits, post-employment life insurance and post-employment medical care.
3. **Other long-term employee benefits:** long-service or sabbatical leave, jubilee and other long-service benefits, long-term disability benefits, and profit-sharing, bonuses and deferred compensation not payable wholly within twelve months.
4. **Termination benefits:** payments on ending employment before normal retirement, such as VRS payments and retrenchment compensation.

## Q2. Explain the scope of AS 15.
AS 15 applies to the accounting by employers for all employee benefits. It does not define the term "employee", but it is applied widely.
1. **All kinds of employees:** full-time, part-time, permanent, casual or temporary staff, including whole-time directors and other management personnel.
2. **No formal relationship needed:** the standard applies to every form of employer-employee relationship. A "contract of service" is covered, but a "contract for services", such as outsourcing, usually is not.
3. **Formal plans and agreements:** benefits under agreements with individual employees, groups or their representatives.
4. **Legal requirements:** benefits that the law or industry arrangements require the enterprise to pay.
5. **Informal practices:** practices that leave the enterprise no realistic alternative but to pay, such as a regular festival lump sum, are covered and must be provided for.

## Q3. Distinguish between a defined contribution plan and a defined benefit plan.
| Basis | Defined contribution plan | Defined benefit plan |
|---|---|---|
| Promise | Fixed contribution to a fund | Agreed amount of benefit |
| Risk | Borne by the employee | Borne by the enterprise |
| Further obligation | None beyond the contribution | Must make good any shortfall |
| Expense | Contribution payable for the year | Worked out actuarially |
| Measurement | No actuarial valuation, no discounting usually | Actuarial valuation and discounting |
| Example | Provident fund | Gratuity, pension |

## Q4. Explain the accounting for short-term employee benefits.
Short-term benefits fall due within twelve months after the end of the period of service. They are measured without discounting and without actuarial assumptions.
1. **Expense:** the undiscounted amount of benefits payable for the service rendered is recognised as an expense, unless another standard allows it to be included in the cost of an asset.
2. **Liability:** any amount unpaid at the year end is shown as a liability. For example, if salary of Rs. 2 crore is due and Rs. 1.90 crore is paid, Rs. 10 lakh is a liability.
3. **Prepayment:** if more than the amount due has been paid, the excess is an asset to the extent it will reduce future payments or be refunded.
4. **Compensated absences:** paid leave is recognised when employees render the service that entitles them to it. Earned leave that can be carried forward is short-term only if it is expected to be used or encashed within the next twelve months; otherwise it is a long-term benefit.
5. **Bonus and profit-sharing:** recognised when there is a present obligation from past events and a reliable estimate can be made.

## Q5. Explain the components of the defined benefit cost charged to profit and loss.
For a defined benefit plan, the obligation is measured by an actuary using the projected unit credit method and discounted to present value. The total of the following is charged to profit and loss, unless another standard allows it to be included in the cost of an asset:
1. **Current service cost:** the increase in the present value of the obligation from employee service in the current period.
2. **Interest cost:** the increase in the present value of the obligation because the benefits are one period closer to settlement.
3. **Expected return on plan assets:** deducted from the cost.
4. **Actuarial gains and losses:** recognised immediately in profit and loss.
5. **Past service cost:** the increase in the obligation for earlier service caused by a new or improved benefit.
6. **Curtailments and settlements:** the gain or loss on reducing or settling the plan.

# AS 15: EMPLOYEE BENEFITS – 10 MARKS

## Q1. What are employee benefits? Explain the types and the accounting for each under AS 15.
**Employee benefits** are all forms of consideration given by an enterprise in exchange for service rendered by employees, including benefits arising from informal practices. The objective of AS 15 is to recognise a liability when an employee has provided service for benefits to be paid in future, and an expense when the enterprise uses that service.
### Short-term employee benefits
1. **Meaning:** wages, salaries, paid leave, bonuses payable within twelve months, and non-monetary benefits for current employees.
2. **Accounting:** the undiscounted amount is expensed in the period of service; any unpaid amount is a liability and any excess payment an asset.
### Post-employment benefits
3. **Meaning:** gratuity, pension, provident fund, post-employment medical care and life insurance.
4. **Defined contribution plan:** the contribution payable for the year is the expense; the enterprise has no further obligation.
5. **Defined benefit plan:** the obligation is valued actuarially by the projected unit credit method and discounted; the cost charged is current service cost, interest cost, less expected return on plan assets, plus actuarial gains and losses, past service cost and the effect of curtailments.
6. **Balance sheet:** the present value of the obligation less the fair value of plan assets is shown as a liability.
### Other long-term employee benefits
7. **Meaning:** long-service and sabbatical leave, long-term disability benefits and deferred bonuses.
8. **Accounting:** measured like a defined benefit plan, but actuarial gains and losses and past service cost are recognised immediately.
### Termination benefits
9. **Meaning:** payments on ending employment before normal retirement or on voluntary retirement.
10. **Accounting:** recognised as a liability and an expense when there is a present obligation from a past event, an outflow is probable, and a reliable estimate can be made.

# AS 16: BORROWING COSTS – 2 MARKS

## Q1. Give the meaning of borrowing cost. (Q.P. 54323)
**Borrowing cost**, as per AS 16, means interest and other costs incurred in connection with the borrowing of funds. It includes interest on loans, amortisation of discount or premium, finance charges under leases, and exchange differences to the extent they are an adjustment to interest.

## Q2. Define "Qualifying Asset" as per AS-16 (Borrowing Costs). (Model Nov 2026)
A **qualifying asset**, as per AS 16, is an asset that necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale. Borrowing costs that are directly attributable to such an asset are capitalised as part of its cost.

## Q3. What is a substantial period of time under AS 16?
A **substantial period of time** depends on the facts of each case. Ordinarily, a period of twelve months is considered substantial, unless a shorter or longer period can be justified by the facts and circumstances.

## Q4. What is the capitalisation rate?
The **capitalisation rate** is the weighted average of the borrowing costs on the enterprise's borrowings outstanding during the period, other than borrowings made specifically for a qualifying asset. It is applied to the expenditure on the asset that is financed by general borrowings.

## Q5. When should capitalisation of borrowing costs cease?
**Capitalisation ceases** when substantially all the activities necessary to prepare the qualifying asset for its intended use or sale are complete. Routine administrative work or minor modifications, such as decoration to the user's specification, do not extend it.

## Q6. Give two examples of assets that are not qualifying assets.
1. **Investments in shares:** they are ready for their intended use when bought, so interest on money borrowed to buy them is charged to profit and loss.
2. **Assets ready when acquired:** for example machinery installed on the date of purchase, or inventories routinely produced in large quantities over a short period.

# AS 16: BORROWING COSTS – 5 MARKS

## Q1. Explain the components of borrowing costs as per AS 16.
Borrowing costs are interest and other costs incurred by an enterprise in connection with the borrowing of funds. They include:
1. **Interest and commitment charges:** on bank borrowings and other short-term and long-term borrowings.
2. **Discounts or premiums:** amortisation of discounts or premiums relating to borrowings.
3. **Ancillary costs:** amortisation of costs incurred in arranging borrowings.
4. **Finance charges:** on assets acquired under finance leases or similar arrangements.
5. **Exchange differences:** on foreign currency borrowings, to the extent they are regarded as an adjustment to interest costs.

## Q2. Explain the borrowing costs eligible for capitalisation.
Borrowing costs directly attributable to the acquisition, construction or production of a qualifying asset are capitalised as part of its cost. Other borrowing costs are recognised as an expense in the period in which they are incurred.
1. **Specific borrowings:** the actual borrowing costs on funds borrowed specifically for the asset are capitalised, less any income from temporary investment of those borrowings.
2. **General borrowings:** where the asset is financed from general funds, the capitalisation rate (weighted average rate of general borrowings) is applied to the expenditure on the asset.
3. **Limit:** the amount capitalised in a period cannot exceed the borrowing costs incurred in that period.
4. **Mixed use of a loan:** when one loan is used for several purposes, only the interest on the part used for qualifying assets is capitalised; the interest on working capital and non-qualifying assets is expensed.

## Q3. Explain the commencement, suspension and cessation of capitalisation of borrowing costs.
AS 16 lays down when capitalisation starts, when it is interrupted and when it ends.
### Commencement
Capitalisation begins when all three conditions are met:
1. expenditure for the acquisition, construction or production of the asset is being incurred,
2. borrowing costs are being incurred, and
3. activities necessary to prepare the asset for its intended use or sale are in progress.
### Suspension
4. Capitalisation is suspended during extended periods in which active development is interrupted. It is not suspended when substantial technical and administrative work is going on, or when a temporary delay is a necessary part of the process, such as high water levels during the building of a sea-link.
### Cessation
5. Capitalisation ceases when substantially all the activities necessary to prepare the asset for its intended use or sale are complete.
6. When an asset is completed in parts and a completed part can be used while work continues on other parts, capitalisation ceases for that part when it is ready.

## Q4. What disclosures are required by AS 16?
AS 16 requires two disclosures in the financial statements:
1. **Accounting policy:** the policy adopted for borrowing costs.
2. **Amount capitalised:** the amount of borrowing costs capitalised during the period.

## Q5. How is capitalisation treated when a qualifying asset is completed in parts?
The cessation of capitalisation depends on the nature of the qualifying asset when it consists of several parts.
1. **Parts usable separately:** where each part can be used while construction continues on other parts, capitalisation ceases for a part when that part is ready. For example, in an entertainment park whose rides operate independently, capitalisation stops for the three-fourth part that is open to the public.
2. **Parts usable only together:** where all parts must be completed before any part can be used, such as a plant with several processes in sequence, capitalisation continues until the whole asset is complete.
3. **Phases in use:** for a building project in phases, interest relating to phases that have become operational is capitalised only up to that date and expensed after it, while interest on unfinished phases stays in capital work-in-progress.

# AS 16: BORROWING COSTS – 10 MARKS

## Q1. Explain AS 16 on borrowing costs.
**Borrowing costs** are interest and other costs incurred by an enterprise in connection with the borrowing of funds. AS 16 decides which of these costs are added to the cost of an asset and which are charged to profit and loss.
### Meaning
1. **Components:** interest and commitment charges, amortisation of discounts, premiums and ancillary costs, finance charges on finance leases, and exchange differences that are an adjustment to interest.
2. **Qualifying asset:** an asset that necessarily takes a substantial period of time, ordinarily twelve months, to get ready for its intended use or sale, such as a factory building or power plant. Investments and assets ready when acquired are not qualifying assets.
### Recognition
3. **Capitalisation:** borrowing costs directly attributable to a qualifying asset are capitalised; other borrowing costs are expensed as incurred.
4. **Specific borrowings:** actual borrowing costs less income on temporary investment of those funds.
5. **General borrowings:** the capitalisation rate, the weighted average rate of general borrowings, applied to the expenditure financed by them, not exceeding the borrowing costs of the period.
### Period of capitalisation
6. **Commencement:** when expenditure and borrowing costs are being incurred and the activities to prepare the asset are in progress.
7. **Suspension:** during extended periods when active development is interrupted, but not for necessary temporary delays.
8. **Cessation:** when substantially all activities to prepare the asset are complete; for an asset in usable parts, part by part.
### Disclosure
9. **Policy:** the accounting policy adopted for borrowing costs.
10. **Amount:** the amount of borrowing costs capitalised during the period.

# AS 19: LEASES – 2 MARKS

## Q1. What is a lease? (Q.P. 54323)
A **lease**, as per AS 19, is an agreement by which the lessor gives the lessee the right to use an asset for an agreed period, in return for a payment or a series of payments. A finance lease transfers substantially all the risks and rewards of ownership. An operating lease does not.

## Q2. Distinguish between a Finance Lease and an Operating Lease under AS-19. (Model Nov 2026)
A **finance lease** transfers substantially all the risks and rewards incidental to ownership from the lessor to the lessee. An **operating lease** does not. One test is that the present value of the minimum lease payments amounts to substantially all of the fair value of the asset.

## Q3. What is the interest rate implicit in the lease?
The **interest rate implicit in the lease** is the discount rate that, at the inception of the lease, makes the present value of the minimum lease payments (from the lessor's side) and any unguaranteed residual value accruing to the lessor equal to the fair value of the leased asset.

## Q4. What are minimum lease payments?
**Minimum lease payments**, from the lessee's side, are the lease rents plus any residual value guaranteed by the lessee. The unguaranteed residual value is not part of the minimum lease payments.

## Q5. What is unearned finance income?
**Unearned finance income** is the difference between the lessor's gross investment in the lease and the present value of that gross investment. Gross investment = minimum lease payments + unguaranteed residual value.

## Q6. What is a sale and leaseback transaction?
A **sale and leaseback** is a transaction in which the owner sells an asset and then leases the same asset back from the buyer. The lease payment and the sale price are usually negotiated together, and the treatment of profit or loss depends on whether the leaseback is a finance lease or an operating lease.

## Q7. What is gross investment in a lease?
**Gross investment** in a lease is the total of the minimum lease payments under a finance lease from the lessor's side and any unguaranteed residual value accruing to the lessor.

## Q8. Distinguish between guaranteed and unguaranteed residual value.
1. **Guaranteed residual value:** the part of the residual value of the leased asset that the lessee (or a party related to him) guarantees to the lessor. It forms part of the minimum lease payments.
2. **Unguaranteed residual value:** the part of the residual value that is not guaranteed. It is not part of the minimum lease payments, but it is included in the lessor's gross investment.

# AS 19: LEASES – 5 MARKS

## Q1. Explain the types of lease as per AS 19.
For accounting, AS 19 classifies leases into two categories, finance lease and operating lease.
### Finance lease
A finance lease transfers substantially all the risks and rewards incidental to ownership of an asset to the lessee, though not the legal ownership. A lease is normally a finance lease when:
1. the lessee gets ownership of the asset at the end of the lease term,
2. the lessee has an option to buy the asset at a price lower than its expected fair value when the option is exercised, and it is reasonably certain to be exercised,
3. the lease term covers the major part of the economic life of the asset, even if title is not transferred,
4. at the inception, the present value of the minimum lease payments is substantially all of the fair value of the asset, or
5. the asset is of a specialised nature, so that only the lessee can use it without major modification.
### Operating lease
6. An operating lease is a lease other than a finance lease. It does not transfer substantially all the risks and rewards incidental to ownership.

## Q2. Distinguish between a finance lease and an operating lease.
| Basis | Finance lease | Operating lease |
|---|---|---|
| Risks and rewards | Substantially all pass to the lessee | Remain with the lessor |
| Lease term | Covers the major part of the asset's life | Usually a short part of the life |
| Asset shown by | Lessee, as asset and liability | Lessor, as its own fixed asset |
| Depreciation | Charged by the lessee | Charged by the lessor |
| Lease payment in lessee's books | Split into finance charge and repayment of liability | Expense in profit and loss |
| Cancellation | Usually non-cancellable | Often cancellable |

## Q3. Explain the accounting treatment for a sale and leaseback transaction under an operating lease.
Where a sale and leaseback results in an operating lease, the seller-lessee treats the profit or loss on the sale as follows:
1. **Sale price equals fair value:** profit or loss is recognised immediately.
2. **Sale price below fair value:** profit is recognised immediately. Loss is also recognised immediately, unless it is compensated by future lease payments below market price, in which case it is deferred and amortised in proportion to the lease payments.
3. **Sale price above fair value:** the excess over fair value is deferred and amortised over the period for which the asset is expected to be used.
4. **Fair value below carrying amount:** the loss equal to the difference between carrying amount and fair value is recognised immediately.

## Q4. What are the disclosure requirements for operating leases by the lessee as per AS 19?
AS 19 requires the lessee to disclose the following for operating leases:
1. **Future minimum lease payments:** under non-cancellable operating leases, for not later than one year, later than one year and not later than five years, and later than five years.
2. **Sublease payments:** the total future minimum sublease payments expected to be received under non-cancellable subleases.
3. **Lease payments recognised:** in profit and loss for the period, with separate amounts for minimum lease payments and contingent rents.
4. **Sublease income:** sub-lease payments received or receivable recognised in profit and loss.
5. **Leasing arrangements:** a general description of significant leasing arrangements, including the basis of contingent rent, renewal or purchase options and escalation clauses, and restrictions imposed by the lease on dividends, additional debt and further leasing.

## Q5. Explain the accounting for a finance lease in the books of the lessee.
At the inception of a finance lease, the lessee recognises the lease as an asset and a liability.
1. **Initial amount:** the fair value of the leased asset; if the present value of the minimum lease payments is lower, that lower amount.
2. **Discount rate:** the interest rate implicit in the lease; if it cannot be determined, the lessee's incremental borrowing rate.
3. **Lease payments:** each payment is split into a finance charge and a reduction of the outstanding liability.
4. **Finance charge:** allocated over the lease term so as to give a constant periodic rate of interest on the remaining liability.
5. **Depreciation:** the leased asset is depreciated on the same basis as the lessee's own assets.

# AS 19: LEASES – 10 MARKS

## Q1. What is a lease? Explain the classification of leases and their accounting treatment under AS 19.
A **lease** is an agreement by which the lessor gives the lessee the right to use an asset for an agreed period, in return for a payment or a series of payments. AS 19 does not apply to leases to explore for natural resources such as oil, gas, timber and metals, to licensing agreements for films, video recordings, plays, manuscripts, patents and copyrights, or to leases of land.
### Classification
1. **Finance lease:** transfers substantially all the risks and rewards of ownership to the lessee.
2. **Operating lease:** any lease that is not a finance lease.
### Tests of a finance lease
3. **Transfer of ownership:** ownership passes to the lessee at the end of the lease term.
4. **Bargain purchase option:** the lessee can buy the asset at a price lower than its expected fair value, and is reasonably certain to do so.
5. **Major part of life:** the lease term covers the major part of the economic life of the asset.
6. **Present value test:** the present value of the minimum lease payments is substantially all of the fair value of the asset.
7. **Specialised asset:** only the lessee can use the asset without major modification.
### Finance lease
8. **Lessee:** records an asset and a liability at the lower of fair value and the present value of minimum lease payments, discounted at the implicit rate.
9. **Lessee's payments:** split into finance charge and repayment of liability; the asset is depreciated like owned assets.
10. **Lessor:** shows a receivable equal to the net investment in the lease; unearned finance income (gross investment less its present value) is recognised over the lease term at a constant periodic rate of return.
### Operating lease
11. **Lessee:** lease payments are recognised as an expense in profit and loss, usually on a straight-line basis over the lease term.
12. **Lessor:** keeps the asset as its own fixed asset, depreciates it, and recognises lease income over the lease term, for example in proportion to output where that suits the asset.
### Sale and leaseback
13. **Finance leaseback:** any excess of sale price over carrying amount is deferred and amortised over the lease term.
14. **Operating leaseback:** at fair value, profit or loss is immediate; above fair value, the excess is deferred; below fair value, a loss compensated by low future rents is deferred.

# AS 26: INTANGIBLE ASSETS – 2 MARKS

## Q1. What is an Intangible Asset according to AS-26? (Model Nov 2026)
An **intangible asset**, as per AS 26, is an identifiable non-monetary asset without physical substance, held for use in production or supply, for rental to others, or for administration. Three features are required: identifiability, control, and probable future economic benefits.

## Q2. State any four intangible assets. (Q.P. 54323)
Intangible assets have no physical substance. Four examples are:
1. Patents
2. Copyrights
3. Trademarks
4. Computer software

## Q3. What is amortisation of an intangible asset?
**Amortisation** is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of an intangible asset over its useful life. Depreciable amount is the cost of the asset less its residual value.

## Q4. What is research under AS 26?
**Research** is original and planned investigation undertaken with the prospect of gaining new scientific or technical knowledge and understanding. No intangible asset arises from research; research expenditure is recognised as an expense when incurred.

## Q5. What is development under AS 26?
**Development** is the application of research findings or other knowledge to a plan or design for the production of new or substantially improved materials, devices, products, processes, systems or services, before commercial production or use begins. Development expenditure is capitalised only when six conditions are met.

## Q6. Why is internally generated goodwill not recognised as an asset?
**Internally generated goodwill** is not recognised because it is not an identifiable resource controlled by the enterprise that can be measured reliably at cost. The difference between the market value of an enterprise and its net assets is not the cost of any intangible asset.

## Q7. What is an active market?
An **active market** is a market in which the items traded are homogeneous, willing buyers and sellers can normally be found at any time, and prices are available to the public.

## Q8. What is the residual value of an intangible asset?
**Residual value** is the amount an enterprise expects to obtain for an asset at the end of its useful life, after deducting the expected costs of disposal. For an intangible asset it is taken as zero, unless a third party has committed to buy the asset or there is an active market for it.

## Q9. What is meant by control over an intangible asset?
An enterprise **controls** an asset when it has the power to obtain the future economic benefits from it and to restrict the access of others to those benefits. Control usually comes from legal rights, so staff skills and customer loyalty are not intangible assets.

# AS 26: INTANGIBLE ASSETS – 5 MARKS

## Q1. What is meant by amortisation of an intangible asset? What are the different methods of amortisation as per AS 26? (Q.P. 54323)
**Amortisation** is the systematic writing off of the depreciable amount of an intangible asset over its useful life. Depreciable amount is cost, or the amount substituted for cost, less any residual value. The charge begins when the asset is available for use and is recognised as an expense.

AS 26 requires the method to follow the pattern in which the economic benefits are consumed. If that pattern cannot be determined reliably, the straight-line method is used. The methods are:
1. **Straight-line method:** an equal amount each year.
2. **Diminishing-balance method:** a fixed percentage on the written-down value.
3. **Unit-of-production method:** based on the units expected from the asset.

The period and the method are reviewed at each financial year end. A change is treated as a change in accounting estimate.

## Q2. What is an intangible asset? State the conditions for its recognition under AS 26.
An **intangible asset** is an identifiable non-monetary asset, without physical substance, held for use in the production or supply of goods or services, for rental to others, or for administrative purposes. An item must meet three key features of the definition:
1. **Identifiability:** the asset can be clearly distinguished from goodwill, for example because it is separable or arises from legal rights.
2. **Control:** the enterprise can obtain the benefits and restrict others' access to them, usually through legal rights.
3. **Future economic benefits:** revenue, cost savings or other benefits from using the asset are expected.

It is then recognised if, and only if:
4. it is probable that the future economic benefits attributable to the asset will flow to the enterprise, and
5. the cost of the asset can be measured reliably.

An intangible asset is measured initially at cost. If an item does not meet the definition, the expenditure on it is expensed when incurred.

## Q3. What is the cost of an intangible asset acquired separately?
If an intangible asset is acquired separately, its cost can usually be measured reliably, especially when the price is paid in cash. The cost comprises:
1. **Purchase price:** the price paid, after deducting trade discounts and rebates.
2. **Duties and taxes:** import duties and other taxes, other than those later recoverable from the tax authorities.
3. **Directly attributable expenditure:** costs of making the asset ready for its intended use, such as professional fees for legal services.
4. **Exchange for shares:** if acquired for shares or other securities, the asset is recorded at its fair value or the fair value of the securities issued, whichever is more clearly evident.

## Q4. State the conditions for recognition of an intangible asset arising from development.
An intangible asset arising from development, or from the development phase of an internal project, is recognised if, and only if, the enterprise can show all of the following:
1. **Technical feasibility:** of completing the asset so that it will be available for use or sale.
2. **Intention:** to complete the asset and use or sell it.
3. **Ability:** to use or sell the asset.
4. **Future benefits:** how the asset will generate probable future economic benefits, for example a market for its output or its usefulness inside the enterprise.
5. **Resources:** adequate technical, financial and other resources to complete the development and use or sell the asset.
6. **Reliable measurement:** of the expenditure on the asset during its development.

## Q5. Explain the scope of AS 26.
AS 26 prescribes the accounting for intangible assets that are not dealt with in another standard. It applies to all enterprises, except for:
1. **Assets under other standards:** intangibles held for sale in the ordinary course of business (AS 2 and AS 7), deferred tax assets (AS 22), leases under AS 19, and goodwill on amalgamation (AS 14) or consolidation (AS 21).
2. **Financial assets.**
3. **Extractive rights:** mineral rights and expenditure on exploration, development and extraction of minerals, oil, natural gas and similar non-regenerative resources.
4. **Insurance contracts:** intangibles arising in insurance enterprises from contracts with policyholders.
5. **Termination benefits:** expenditure on termination benefits.

AS 26 does apply to expenditure on advertising, training, start-up and research and development, and to rights under licensing agreements for films, video recordings, plays, manuscripts, patents and copyrights.

## Q6. Which expenditure on intangible items is recognised as an expense?
Expenditure on an intangible item is expensed when incurred, unless it forms part of the cost of an intangible asset that meets the recognition criteria, or is part of goodwill acquired in an amalgamation. Examples of expenditure always expensed are:
1. **Research:** expenditure on research or on the research phase.
2. **Start-up costs:** preliminary expenses, pre-opening and pre-operating costs, unless included in a fixed asset under AS 10.
3. **Training:** expenditure on training activities.
4. **Advertising:** expenditure on advertising and promotional activities.
5. **Relocation:** expenditure on relocating or reorganising part or all of an enterprise.
6. **Internally generated brands:** brands, mastheads, publishing titles and customer lists, and later expenditure on them.

Expenditure once expensed cannot later be reinstated as part of the cost of an intangible asset.

## Q7. What factors are considered in determining the useful life of an intangible asset?
AS 26 presumes that the useful life of an intangible asset does not exceed ten years from the date it is available for use. Many factors are considered in estimating the useful life:
1. **Expected usage** of the asset, and whether another management team could manage it efficiently.
2. **Product life cycles** and public information on the lives of similar assets.
3. **Obsolescence:** technical, technological or other.
4. **Stability of the industry** and changes in market demand for the output.
5. **Expected actions** of competitors.
6. **Maintenance expenditure** needed to obtain the benefits, and the ability to incur it.
7. **Legal limits:** the period of control and limits such as expiry of leases or legal rights.
8. **Dependence** on the useful life of other assets.

If a longer life is justified, the asset is amortised over that life, its recoverable amount is estimated every year, and the reasons are disclosed.

## Q8. Distinguish between research and development.
| Basis | Research | Development |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Original, planned investigation to gain new knowledge | Applying research findings to a plan or design for new or improved products |
| Stage | Earlier stage | Later stage, before commercial production |
| Treatment | Always expensed when incurred | Capitalised when all six conditions are met |
| Asset | No intangible asset is recognised | Intangible asset may be recognised |
| Examples | Search for new knowledge or alternative materials | Prototypes, pilot plants, tools and moulds with new technology |

## Q9. What disclosures are required by AS 26?
For each class of intangible assets, separating internally generated assets from others, the financial statements disclose:
1. **Useful lives** or the amortisation rates used.
2. **Amortisation methods** used.
3. **Gross carrying amount** and accumulated amortisation, with accumulated impairment losses, at the beginning and end of the period.
4. **Reconciliation** of the carrying amount, showing additions (separately from internal development and through amalgamation), retirements and disposals, impairment losses recognised and reversed, amortisation and other changes.
5. **Other disclosures:** reasons for a useful life over ten years, details of any material asset, assets with restricted title or pledged as security, commitments to acquire intangible assets, and the total research and development expenditure expensed during the period.

# AS 26: INTANGIBLE ASSETS – 10 MARKS

## Q1. Explain the recognition and measurement of internally generated intangible assets as per AS 26.
It is often hard to say whether, and from when, an internally generated item will give probable future benefits, and to separate its cost from the cost of running the business. So AS 26 divides the generation of the asset into a research phase and a development phase. If the two cannot be separated, the whole expenditure is treated as research.
### Research phase
1. **Meaning:** original and planned investigation to gain new scientific or technical knowledge.
2. **Treatment:** no intangible asset is recognised; research expenditure is expensed when incurred.
3. **Examples:** obtaining new knowledge; searching for and evaluating applications of research findings; searching for alternative materials, products or processes.
### Development phase
4. **Meaning:** applying research findings to a plan or design for new or improved products or processes before commercial production.
5. **Conditions:** technical feasibility, intention to complete, ability to use or sell, probable future benefits, adequate resources, and reliable measurement of the expenditure.
6. **Examples:** design and testing of prototypes and models, tools, jigs, moulds and dies with new technology, and pilot plants not of commercial scale.
### Cost of the asset
7. **Starting point:** cost is the expenditure incurred from the date the asset first meets the recognition criteria; earlier expensed amounts are never reinstated.
8. **Included:** materials and services used, salaries of staff directly engaged, directly attributable costs such as fees to register a legal right, and overheads allocated on a reasonable and consistent basis.
9. **Excluded:** selling, administrative and general overheads, identified inefficiencies and initial operating losses, and staff training costs.
### Goodwill and brands
10. **Not recognised:** internally generated goodwill, brands, mastheads, publishing titles and customer lists, because their cost cannot be separated from developing the business as a whole.

## Q2. Explain the measurement of intangible assets after recognition, amortisation, retirement and disclosure under AS 26.
After initial recognition, an intangible asset is carried at its cost less any accumulated amortisation and any accumulated impairment losses.
### Amortisation
1. **Period:** the depreciable amount is allocated systematically over the best estimate of useful life, starting when the asset is available for use.
2. **Ten-year presumption:** the useful life is presumed not to exceed ten years; a longer life needs evidence, annual testing of recoverable amount and disclosure of reasons.
3. **Legal rights:** the life cannot exceed the period of legal rights unless renewal is virtually certain.
4. **Method:** straight-line, diminishing balance or unit of production, matching the pattern of benefits; straight-line if the pattern is not reliable.
5. **Residual value:** taken as zero unless a third party has agreed to buy the asset or an active market exists.
6. **Review:** the period and method are reviewed at least at each financial year end; changes are treated as changes in accounting estimate under AS 5.
### Impairment and retirement
7. **Impairment:** tested under AS 28; recoverable amount is estimated every year for assets not yet available for use and for those amortised over more than ten years.
8. **Retirement and disposal:** the asset is derecognised on disposal or when no future benefits are expected; the gain or loss is net disposal proceeds less carrying amount.
### Disclosure
9. **For each class:** useful lives or rates, methods, gross carrying amount and accumulated amortisation, and a reconciliation of the carrying amount.
10. **Life over ten years:** the reasons, and the factors that decided the useful life.
11. **Material assets:** a description, carrying amount and remaining amortisation period of any material individual asset.
12. **Restricted and pledged assets:** assets whose title is restricted, assets pledged as security, and commitments to acquire intangible assets.
13. **Research and development:** the total research and development expenditure expensed during the period.

# AS 28: IMPAIRMENT OF ASSETS – 2 MARKS

## Q1. What is an impairment loss?
An **impairment loss** is the amount by which the carrying amount of an asset, or of a cash-generating unit, exceeds its recoverable amount. It is recognised as an expense in the statement of profit and loss immediately.

## Q2. What is recoverable amount?
**Recoverable amount** is the higher of an asset's net selling price and its value in use. If either amount exceeds the carrying amount, the asset is not impaired.

## Q3. What is net selling price?
**Net selling price** is the amount obtainable from the sale of an asset in an arm's length transaction between knowledgeable, willing parties, less the costs of disposal. Costs of disposal exclude finance costs and income tax expense.

## Q4. What is value in use?
**Value in use** is the present value of the estimated future cash flows expected to arise from the continuing use of an asset and from its disposal at the end of its useful life.

## Q5. What is a cash-generating unit?
A **cash-generating unit** is the smallest identifiable group of assets that generates cash inflows from continuing use that are largely independent of the cash inflows from other assets or groups of assets. An example is a mine as a whole, when its private railway cannot generate cash by itself.

## Q6. What are corporate assets?
**Corporate assets** are assets, other than goodwill, that do not generate cash inflows independently and whose carrying amount cannot be fully attributed to the cash-generating unit under review. Examples are a head office building, EDP equipment and a research centre.

## Q7. What is carrying amount?
**Carrying amount** is the amount at which an asset is recognised in the balance sheet after deducting any accumulated depreciation (amortisation) and accumulated impairment losses.

# AS 28: IMPAIRMENT OF ASSETS – 5 MARKS

## Q1. Write a short note on impairment of assets and its application to inventory.
The objective of AS 28 is to ensure that the assets of an enterprise are carried at no more than their recoverable amount. An asset is impaired when its carrying amount exceeds the amount to be recovered through its use or sale, and the standard then requires an impairment loss to be recognised.
1. **Not impaired:** carrying amount is equal to or less than recoverable amount.
2. **Impaired:** carrying amount is more than recoverable amount; impairment loss = carrying amount − recoverable amount.
3. **Recoverable amount:** the higher of net selling price and value in use.
4. **Inventories excluded:** AS 28 does not apply to inventories (AS 2), assets from construction contracts (AS 7), financial assets including investments under AS 13, and deferred tax assets (AS 22).
5. **Reason:** those standards already have their own rules for measuring such assets, for example inventory is written down to net realisable value under AS 2.

## Q2. Explain the indications of impairment of assets.
At each balance sheet date an enterprise assesses whether there is any indication that an asset may be impaired. If there is, it estimates the recoverable amount. As a minimum, it considers the following:
### External sources
1. **Market value decline:** the asset's market value has fallen much more than expected from the passage of time or normal use.
2. **Adverse changes:** significant changes in the technological, market, economic or legal environment.
3. **Interest rates:** a rise in market interest rates that increases the discount rate and lowers the recoverable amount materially.
4. **Market capitalisation:** the carrying amount of the enterprise's net assets is more than its market capitalisation.
### Internal sources
5. **Obsolescence or damage:** evidence of obsolescence or physical damage of an asset.
6. **Change in use:** plans to discontinue or restructure the operation, or to dispose of the asset earlier than expected.
7. **Poor performance:** internal reports show that the economic performance of the asset is, or will be, worse than expected.

## Q3. Explain the recognition and measurement of an impairment loss.
An impairment loss is the amount by which the carrying amount of an asset exceeds its recoverable amount.
1. **Recoverable amount more than carrying amount:** there is no impairment and the asset continues at its book value, though its useful life, depreciation method or residual value may need review under AS 10.
2. **Recoverable amount less than carrying amount:** the difference is an impairment loss and the asset is written down to its recoverable amount.
3. **Charge to profit and loss:** the loss is written off immediately as an expense.
4. **Revalued asset:** the loss is first adjusted against the revaluation surplus of that asset, and any balance is charged to profit and loss.
5. **Depreciation afterwards:** future depreciation is based on the new carrying amount, residual value and remaining useful life.
6. **Loss more than carrying amount:** a liability is recognised only if another standard requires it.

## Q4. Explain the reversal of an impairment loss.
At each balance sheet date an enterprise assesses whether an impairment loss recognised earlier may no longer exist or may have decreased. If so, it estimates the recoverable amount again. A loss is reversed only if there has been a change in the estimates used to find the recoverable amount.
1. **Individual asset, loss charged to profit and loss:** the reversal is recognised as income immediately.
2. **Individual asset, loss adjusted against revaluation surplus:** the reversal is written back to the surplus to that extent, and any balance is income.
3. **Ceiling:** the increased carrying amount cannot exceed the carrying amount (net of depreciation) that would have existed had no impairment loss been recognised.
4. **Cash-generating unit:** the reversal goes first to assets other than goodwill, pro rata to their carrying amounts, and then to goodwill.
5. **Goodwill:** a loss on goodwill is reversed only if it was caused by a specific external event of an exceptional nature not expected to recur, and later external events have reversed its effect.
6. **Depreciation afterwards:** future depreciation is based on the revised carrying amount over the remaining useful life.

## Q5. How is an impairment loss of a cash-generating unit allocated?
When the recoverable amount of a cash-generating unit is less than its carrying amount, the impairment loss is allocated to reduce the carrying amounts of its assets in this order:
1. **Goodwill first:** to any goodwill allocated to the unit.
2. **Other assets next:** to the other assets of the unit, pro rata to their carrying amounts.
3. **Floor:** no asset is reduced below the highest of its net selling price, its value in use and zero; the amount not allocated to it goes to the other assets pro rata.
4. **Goodwill and corporate assets:** if they can be allocated to the unit on a reasonable and consistent basis, only the bottom-up test is applied; if not, both the bottom-up and top-down tests are applied.
5. **Remaining loss:** a liability is recognised for any balance only if another standard requires it.

## Q6. Explain the scope of AS 28.
AS 28 applies to the impairment of all assets, except those for which other standards contain specific rules:
1. **Inventories** (AS 2).
2. **Assets arising under construction contracts** (AS 7).
3. **Financial assets**, including investments covered by AS 13.
4. **Deferred tax assets** (AS 22).

The requirements apply equally to an individual asset and to a cash-generating unit. Provisions for impairment losses may weaken companies that are already near sickness.

## Q7. What disclosures are required by AS 28?
For each class of assets the financial statements disclose:
1. **Losses in profit and loss:** the impairment losses recognised in the statement of profit and loss, and the line items that include them.
2. **Reversals in profit and loss:** the reversals of impairment losses recognised, and the line items.
3. **Against revaluation surplus:** impairment losses recognised directly against revaluation surplus.
4. **Reversals in revaluation surplus:** reversals recognised directly in revaluation surplus.
5. **Material losses:** for a material loss or reversal, the events that led to it, the amount, the nature of the asset or a description of the unit, whether recoverable amount is net selling price or value in use, and the basis or discount rate used.

# AS 28: IMPAIRMENT OF ASSETS – 10 MARKS

## Q1. What is impairment of assets? Explain the indications of impairment and the measurement of recoverable amount under AS 28.
AS 28 prescribes the procedures that ensure the assets of an enterprise are carried at no more than their recoverable amount. An asset is impaired when its carrying amount exceeds its recoverable amount, and the excess is the **impairment loss**.
### Indications of impairment: external sources
1. **Market value:** a fall in the asset's market value much more than expected from time or normal use.
2. **Environment:** significant adverse changes in the technological, market, economic or legal environment.
3. **Interest rates:** a rise in market rates that lowers the recoverable amount materially.
4. **Market capitalisation:** net assets of the enterprise more than its market capitalisation.
### Indications of impairment: internal sources
5. **Obsolescence or damage:** evidence of obsolescence or physical damage of the asset.
6. **Change in use:** plans to discontinue or restructure the operation, or to dispose of the asset early.
7. **Poor performance:** internal reports show that the asset's economic performance is, or will be, worse than expected.
### Measurement of recoverable amount
8. **Recoverable amount:** the higher of net selling price and value in use; if either exceeds the carrying amount, the other need not be found.
9. **Net selling price:** price in a binding sale agreement or an active market, or the best estimate available, less costs of disposal (excluding finance costs and income tax).
10. **Value in use:** present value of future cash flows from continuing use and from disposal at the end of the useful life.
11. **Cash flow estimates:** based on recent approved budgets for a maximum of five years unless a longer period is justified; they exclude financing cash flows, income tax, future restructuring and capacity-enhancing expenditure.
12. **Discount rate:** a pre-tax rate that reflects current market assessment of the time value of money and the risks specific to the asset.

## Q2. Explain Accounting Standard 28 on impairment of assets.
**AS 28** makes sure that assets are not carried at more than their recoverable amount. The enterprise assesses at each balance sheet date whether any asset may be impaired, and if so estimates its recoverable amount and recognises any impairment loss in profit and loss.
### Scope and assessment
1. **Scope:** all assets except inventories, construction-contract assets, financial assets including investments, and deferred tax assets.
2. **Assessment:** external and internal indications are checked at each balance sheet date.
### Measurement and recognition
3. **Recoverable amount:** the higher of net selling price and value in use.
4. **Impairment loss:** carrying amount less recoverable amount, charged to profit and loss, or first against revaluation surplus for a revalued asset.
5. **After the loss:** depreciation is recalculated on the new carrying amount over the remaining life.
### Cash-generating units
6. **Meaning:** the smallest group of assets that generates largely independent cash inflows; used when an asset's recoverable amount cannot be found alone.
7. **Goodwill and corporate assets:** tested with the unit, by the bottom-up test, and also the top-down test where they cannot be allocated reasonably.
8. **Allocation of loss:** first to goodwill, then to other assets pro rata, but no asset below the highest of net selling price, value in use and zero.
### Reversal and disclosure
9. **Reversal:** allowed when the estimates change, up to the carrying amount that would have existed without the loss; for a unit, to other assets first and goodwill last; goodwill only for exceptional external events.
10. **Disclosure:** losses and reversals in profit and loss and in revaluation surplus for each class, and for material items the events, amounts and basis of recoverable amount.

# AS 29: PROVISIONS, CONTINGENT LIABILITIES AND CONTINGENT ASSETS – 2 MARKS

## Q1. Write a note on AS 29. (Q.P. 54323)
**AS 29** lays down when a provision, a contingent liability and a contingent asset are recognised. A provision is recorded only when a present obligation arises from a past event, an outflow of resources is probable, and the amount can be estimated reliably. A contingent liability is disclosed, not recognised. A contingent asset is not recognised.

## Q2. Define a Provision as per AS-29. (Model Nov 2026)
A **provision**, as per AS 29, is a liability that can be measured only by using a substantial degree of estimation. It is recognised only when a present obligation arises from a past event, an outflow of resources is probable, and a reliable estimate can be made.

## Q3. What is a contingent liability?
A **contingent liability** is either a possible obligation whose existence will be confirmed only by uncertain future events outside the enterprise's control, or a present obligation not recognised because an outflow is not probable or the amount cannot be estimated reliably. It is not recognised but is disclosed in the notes, unless the possibility of outflow is remote.

## Q4. What is a contingent asset?
A **contingent asset** is a possible asset arising from past events whose existence will be confirmed only by uncertain future events not wholly within the enterprise's control. It is neither recognised nor disclosed in the financial statements; it is recognised only when the inflow becomes virtually certain.

## Q5. What is an obligating event?
An **obligating event** is an event that creates a legal or constructive obligation, leaving the enterprise no realistic alternative to settling that obligation.

## Q6. What is an onerous contract?
An **onerous contract** is a contract in which the unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations exceed the economic benefits expected from it. The provision is the lower of the cost of fulfilling the contract and the penalty for not fulfilling it.

## Q7. How is a provision measured under AS 29?
A provision is measured at the **best estimate** of the expenditure required to settle the present obligation at the balance sheet date. Risks and uncertainties are taken into account, and under AS 29 provisions are not discounted to present value.

# AS 29: PROVISIONS, CONTINGENT LIABILITIES AND CONTINGENT ASSETS – 5 MARKS

## Q1. When should a provision be recognised as per AS 29? Explain in brief. (Q.P. 54323)
AS 29 says a provision is recognised only when all three conditions are met:
1. The enterprise has a present obligation, legal or constructive, as a result of a past event.
2. An outflow of resources embodying economic benefits is probable, that is more likely than not.
3. A reliable estimate can be made of the amount. The amount recognised is the best estimate of the expenditure required to settle the obligation at the balance-sheet date.

If any one condition fails, no provision is made. The item is then a contingent liability and is disclosed, unless the possibility of outflow is remote. A contingent asset is not recognised.

## Q2. Explain the objective and scope of AS 29.
The objective of AS 29 is to ensure that proper recognition criteria and measurement bases are applied to provisions, contingent liabilities and contingent assets, and that enough information is disclosed in the notes for users to understand them. AS 29 does not apply to:
1. **Financial instruments** carried at fair value.
2. **Executory contracts**, unless they are onerous.
3. **Insurance contracts** of insurance enterprises with policyholders.
4. **Items covered by other standards**, such as construction contracts (AS 7), employee benefits (AS 15), leases (AS 19) and taxes on income (AS 22).

## Q3. Explain the treatment of contingent liabilities and contingent assets.
Contingent liabilities and contingent assets are not recognised in the balance sheet, because their existence or outflow is uncertain.
### Contingent liabilities
1. **Meaning:** a possible obligation confirmed only by uncertain future events, or a present obligation where the outflow is not probable or cannot be estimated reliably.
2. **Not recognised:** they are not recorded in the financial statements.
3. **Disclosed:** they are disclosed in the notes, unless the possibility of outflow is remote.
4. **Reviewed:** they are reassessed each period; if an outflow becomes probable, a provision is recognised.
### Contingent assets
5. **Not recognised or disclosed:** by prudence, they are kept out of the financial statements so that unrealised income is not recognised.
6. **Board's report:** if an inflow is probable, they are disclosed in the report of the approving authority (Board's report).
7. **Virtually certain:** when the inflow becomes virtually certain, the asset and the income are recognised.

## Q4. Distinguish between a provision and a contingent liability.
| Basis | Provision | Contingent liability |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | A liability measured by substantial estimation | A possible obligation, or an obligation not meeting the recognition tests |
| Obligation | Present obligation from a past event | Possible obligation, or present but not probable or not measurable |
| Probability of outflow | Probable (more than 50%) | Not probable |
| Treatment | Recognised in the balance sheet | Not recognised |
| Disclosure | In the financial statements, with movements | In the notes, unless remote |
| Measurement | Best estimate of the expenditure | Estimate of the financial effect, where practicable |

## Q5. Explain the special rules of AS 29 on reimbursements, future operating losses, onerous contracts and restructuring.
AS 29 applies its recognition rules to some common situations as follows:
1. **Reimbursements:** a reimbursement from another party (such as an insurer) is recognised only when it is virtually certain to be received. It is a separate asset, not more than the provision, though the expense may be shown net of it.
2. **Future operating losses:** no provision is made, because they do not meet the definition of a liability. They may indicate impairment under AS 28.
3. **Onerous contracts:** a provision is made for the lower of the cost of fulfilling the contract and the penalty for not fulfilling it.
4. **Restructuring:** a provision is made only when a detailed formal plan exists and a valid expectation has been raised before the balance sheet date. It excludes retraining staff, marketing and investment in new systems.
5. **Use and review:** a provision is used only for the purpose for which it was made, and it is reviewed and adjusted at each balance sheet date.

## Q6. What disclosures are required by AS 29?
AS 29 requires the following disclosures:
1. **For each class of provision:** the carrying amount at the beginning and end of the period.
2. **Movements:** additional provisions made, and amounts used or reversed during the period.
3. **Description:** a brief description of the nature of the obligation and the expected timing of outflows.
4. **Contingent liabilities:** a brief description of each class, unless the possibility of outflow is remote.
5. **Prejudice exemption:** in extremely rare cases where disclosure would seriously prejudice the enterprise in a dispute, full disclosure is not needed, but the general nature of the dispute and the reason for not disclosing are stated.

# AS 29: PROVISIONS, CONTINGENT LIABILITIES AND CONTINGENT ASSETS – 10 MARKS

## Q1. Explain AS 29 on provisions, contingent liabilities and contingent assets.
AS 29 ensures that appropriate recognition criteria and measurement bases are applied to provisions, contingent liabilities and contingent assets, supported by clear note disclosures.
### Definitions
1. **Provision:** a liability that can be measured only by using a substantial degree of estimation.
2. **Liability:** a present obligation from past events, whose settlement requires an outflow of economic benefits.
3. **Obligating event:** an event that creates a legal or constructive obligation with no realistic alternative to settlement.
### Recognition and measurement of provisions
4. **Three conditions:** a present obligation from a past event, a probable outflow (more than 50%), and a reliable estimate. All three must be met.
5. **Best estimate:** the amount is the best estimate of the expenditure to settle the obligation at the balance sheet date, allowing for risks and uncertainties, without discounting.
6. **Special cases:** reimbursements only when virtually certain; no provision for future operating losses; onerous contracts at the lower of cost and penalty; restructuring only with a formal plan.
### Contingencies
7. **Contingent liability:** not recognised; disclosed in the notes unless remote; reviewed every period.
8. **Contingent asset:** not recognised or disclosed in the financial statements; disclosed in the Board's report if probable; recognised when virtually certain.
### Disclosure
9. **Provisions:** opening and closing amounts, additions, amounts used or reversed, and the nature and timing of the obligation, for each class.
10. **Contingent liabilities:** a brief description of each class, unless the possibility of outflow is remote.
11. **Exemption:** where disclosure would seriously prejudice the enterprise in a dispute, only the general nature of the dispute and the reason are given.

## Q2. Distinguish between a provision, a contingent liability and a contingent asset.
| Basis | Provision | Contingent liability | Contingent asset |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Present obligation | Possible obligation, or obligation not meeting the tests | Possible asset |
| Arises from | Past event | Past event | Past event |
| Probability | Probable outflow (more than 50%) | Outflow possible, not probable | Inflow uncertain or probable |
| Measurement | Reliable best estimate | May not be reliably measurable | Not measured |
| Recognition | Recognised in the balance sheet | Not recognised | Not recognised |
| Disclosure | Financial statements, with movements | Notes to accounts | Board's report, if inflow probable |
| If remote or certain | Not applicable | No disclosure if remote | Recognised if virtually certain |
| Concept | Prudence: expected losses provided | Prudence: users warned | Prudence: no unrealised income |
| Review | Reviewed and adjusted each year | Reassessed each year | Reassessed each year |

# AS 2: VALUATION OF INVENTORIES – 2 MARKS

## Q1. What are inventories as per AS 2 (revised)? (Q.P. 54323)
**Inventories**, as per AS 2 (Revised), are assets held:
1. for sale in the ordinary course of business,
2. in the process of production for such sale, or
3. as materials or supplies to be consumed in production or in rendering services.

## Q2. What is Net Realisable Value (NRV) in inventory valuation as per AS-2? (Model Nov 2026)
**Net realisable value**, as per AS 2, is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business, less the estimated costs of completion and the estimated costs necessary to make the sale. Inventory is carried at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower.

## Q3. What does the cost of inventories comprise?
The **cost of inventories** comprises all costs of purchase, costs of conversion, and other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition.

## Q4. What is meant by normal capacity in AS 2?
**Normal capacity** is the production expected to be achieved on average over a number of periods under normal circumstances. Fixed production overheads are absorbed into inventory on the basis of normal capacity, so that the cost of unused capacity is not carried in inventory.

## Q5. Name the cost formulas allowed by AS 2.
1. **Specific identification:** for items that are not ordinarily interchangeable or are made for specific projects.
2. **FIFO or weighted average cost:** for all other inventories.

# AS 2: VALUATION OF INVENTORIES – 5 MARKS

## Q1. Explain the scope of AS 2.
AS 2 applies to the valuation of inventories, except the following:
1. **Construction work in progress:** work in progress under construction contracts, including directly related service contracts (AS 7).
2. **Service providers:** work in progress arising in the ordinary course of business of service providers.
3. **Financial instruments:** shares, debentures and other financial instruments held as stock-in-trade.
4. **Producers' inventories:** livestock, agricultural and forest products, and mineral oils, ores and gases, to the extent they are measured at net realisable value under established industry practice.

## Q2. Explain the components of the cost of inventories as per AS 2.
Inventories are valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Cost comprises:
1. **Cost of purchase:** purchase price, duties and taxes (other than those later recoverable), freight inwards and other directly attributable costs, less trade discounts, rebates and duty drawbacks.
2. **Cost of conversion:** direct labour and a systematic allocation of fixed and variable production overheads.
3. **Fixed overheads:** allocated on the normal capacity of the facilities.
4. **Variable overheads:** allocated on the actual use of the facilities.
5. **Other costs:** other costs incurred to bring the inventories to their present location and condition.

## Q3. Which costs are excluded from the cost of inventories?
AS 2 excludes costs that do not bring the inventories to their present location and condition. They are expensed in the period incurred:
1. **Abnormal waste:** abnormal amounts of wasted materials, labour or other production costs.
2. **Storage costs:** unless they are necessary in the production process before a further stage.
3. **Administrative overheads:** those that do not help bring the inventories to their present location and condition.
4. **Selling and distribution costs.**

## Q4. Explain the measurement of inventories at the lower of cost and net realisable value.
Inventories are valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value, so that assets are not carried at more than the amount expected to be realised.
1. **Net realisable value:** estimated selling price less estimated costs of completion and costs to make the sale.
2. **Item by item:** inventories are usually written down item by item, or by groups of similar items, not as a whole.
3. **Raw materials:** materials held for production are not written down below cost if the finished goods in which they will be used are expected to sell at or above cost.
4. **Reasons for write-down:** damage, obsolescence, a fall in selling prices or a rise in costs of completion.
5. **Review:** net realisable value is reassessed at each balance sheet date.

## Q5. What disclosures are required by AS 2?
The financial statements disclose:
1. **Accounting policies:** the policies adopted in measuring inventories, including the cost formula used.
2. **Carrying amount:** the total carrying amount of inventories.
3. **Classification:** the carrying amount in suitable classes, such as raw materials and components, work in progress, finished goods, stock-in-trade, stores and spares, and loose tools.

# AS 2: VALUATION OF INVENTORIES – 10 MARKS

## Q1. Explain AS 2 on valuation of inventories.
**Inventories** are assets held for sale in the ordinary course of business, in the process of production for such sale, or as materials or supplies to be consumed in production or in rendering services. AS 2 lays down how they are valued and disclosed.
### Scope: AS 2 does not apply to
1. **Construction work in progress:** under construction contracts, including directly related service contracts (AS 7).
2. **Service providers:** work in progress arising in their ordinary course of business.
3. **Financial instruments:** shares, debentures and similar instruments held as stock-in-trade.
4. **Producers' inventories:** livestock, agricultural and forest products, and mineral oils, ores and gases measured at net realisable value by established industry practice.
### Measurement
5. **Basic rule:** inventories are valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value.
6. **Cost of purchase:** price, non-recoverable duties and taxes, freight inwards and other direct costs, less trade discounts, rebates and duty drawbacks.
7. **Cost of conversion:** direct labour and production overheads; fixed overheads on normal capacity, variable overheads on actual use.
8. **Other costs:** costs of bringing the inventories to their present location and condition.
9. **Excluded costs:** abnormal waste, storage costs, unrelated administrative overheads, and selling costs.
### Cost formulas
10. **Specific identification:** for items not ordinarily interchangeable.
11. **FIFO or weighted average:** for other items; standard cost and retail methods may be used if results approximate cost.
### Net realisable value
12. **Meaning and rule:** estimated selling price less costs of completion and sale; write-downs are item by item, and raw materials are not written down if the finished goods sell at or above cost.
### Disclosure
13. **Policies:** the accounting policies adopted, including the cost formula used.
14. **Amounts:** the total carrying amount, and its classification into raw materials, work in progress, finished goods, stock-in-trade, stores and spares, and loose tools.

# AS 13: ACCOUNTING FOR INVESTMENTS – 2 MARKS

## Q1. Mention the types of investments. Differentiate between Current Investments and Long-term Investments according to AS-13. (Q.P. 54323) (Model Nov 2026)
AS 13 classifies investments by the period for which they are held:
1. **Current investment:** readily realisable, and intended to be held for not more than one year from the date of purchase.
2. **Long-term investment:** any investment other than a current investment.

## Q2. What are investments as per AS 13?
**Investments** are assets held by an enterprise for earning income by way of dividends, interest and rentals, for capital appreciation, or for other benefits to the investing enterprise. Assets held as stock-in-trade are not investments.

## Q3. What is an investment property?
An **investment property** is an investment in land or buildings that are not intended to be occupied substantially for use by, or in the operations of, the investing enterprise. It is accounted for as a long-term investment.

## Q4. What is fair value of an investment?
**Fair value** is the amount for which an asset could be exchanged between a knowledgeable, willing buyer and a knowledgeable, willing seller in an arm's length transaction. Market value is generally taken as fair value where an active market exists.

## Q5. What does the cost of an investment include?
The **cost of an investment** includes the purchase price and acquisition charges such as brokerage, fees and duties. Interest on money borrowed to buy shares is not an acquisition charge, so it is not part of the cost.

## Q6. How are bonus shares recorded under AS 13?
**Bonus shares** are received free, so they are recorded at nil cost. The cost of the original shares is spread over the total number of shares held, which reduces the average cost per share.

# AS 13: ACCOUNTING FOR INVESTMENTS – 5 MARKS

## Q1. Explain the valuation of investments as per AS 13.
AS 13 values an investment according to whether it is current or long-term.
1. **Current investments:** carried at the lower of cost and fair value, worked out for each investment or by category, not on an overall basis. Any fall is charged to profit and loss.
2. **Long-term investments:** carried at cost. A provision is made only for a decline in value that is other than temporary.
3. **Investment property:** accounted for as a long-term investment.
4. **Disposal:** the difference between the carrying amount and the net sale proceeds is taken to profit and loss. On a partial sale, the carrying amount is found on the average carrying amount of the total holding.

## Q2. Explain the determination of the cost of investments.
The cost of an investment includes acquisition charges such as brokerage, fees and duties. AS 13 also gives these rules:
1. **Issue of shares:** if acquired by issuing shares or other securities, cost is the fair value of the securities issued.
2. **Exchange of assets:** if acquired in exchange for another asset, cost is the fair value of the asset given up, or of the investment acquired if that is more clearly evident.
3. **Pre-acquisition interest:** interest bought with the investment (cum-interest purchase) is deducted from cost, since it relates to the period before acquisition.
4. **Pre-acquisition dividend:** a dividend received out of profits earned before purchase reduces the cost.
5. **Bonus shares:** recorded at nil cost; the original cost is spread over all the shares held.
6. **Right shares:** recorded at the amount paid for them.

## Q3. Distinguish between current investments and long-term investments.
| Basis | Current investment | Long-term investment |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Readily realisable, held for not more than one year | Any investment other than current |
| Intention | Short-term holding | Holding for a long period |
| Valuation | Lower of cost and fair value | Cost, less permanent decline |
| Fall in value | Every fall charged to profit and loss | Only a decline other than temporary is provided |
| Balance sheet | Current assets | Non-current investments |

## Q4. Explain the rules for reclassification of investments.
An investment may be transferred from one class to another when the intention of holding it changes. AS 13 fixes the value at which it is transferred:
1. **Long-term to current:** transferred at the lower of cost and carrying amount at the date of transfer.
2. **Current to long-term:** transferred at the lower of cost and fair value at the date of transfer.
3. **Purpose:** these rules stop an enterprise from avoiding a write-down, or showing a gain, simply by changing the label of an investment.

## Q5. What disclosures are required by AS 13?
The financial statements disclose:
1. **Accounting policies:** for determining the carrying amount of investments.
2. **Classification:** investments classified as current and long-term.
3. **Income:** interest, dividends and rentals, profits and losses on disposal, and changes in carrying amount of current and long-term investments.
4. **Restrictions:** significant restrictions on the right of ownership, realisability of investments, or remittance of income and sale proceeds.
5. **Quoted and unquoted:** the aggregate amount of quoted investments with their market value, and the aggregate amount of unquoted investments.

# AS 13: ACCOUNTING FOR INVESTMENTS – 10 MARKS

## Q1. Explain AS 13 on accounting for investments.
**Investments** are assets held for earning income by way of dividends, interest and rentals, for capital appreciation, or for other benefits to the investing enterprise. AS 13 deals with their classification, cost, carrying amount, disposal and disclosure.
### Classification
1. **Current investment:** readily realisable and intended to be held for not more than one year from purchase.
2. **Long-term investment:** any investment other than a current investment; investment property is treated as long-term.
### Cost
3. **Cost:** purchase price plus brokerage, fees and duties.
4. **Special cases:** fair value of shares issued or of assets exchanged; pre-acquisition interest and dividends reduce cost; bonus shares at nil cost; right shares at the price paid.
### Carrying amount
5. **Current investments:** lower of cost and fair value, for each investment or by category.
6. **Long-term investments:** cost, with a provision for a decline other than temporary, reversed when the value rises again.
### Disposal and reclassification
7. **Disposal:** the difference between carrying amount and net proceeds goes to profit and loss; on a part sale, average carrying amount is used.
8. **Reclassification:** long-term to current at the lower of cost and carrying amount; current to long-term at the lower of cost and fair value.
### Disclosure
9. **Policies:** the accounting policies for determining the carrying amount of investments.
10. **Classification:** investments classified as current and long-term.
11. **Income:** interest, dividends and rentals, profits and losses on disposal, and changes in carrying amount.
12. **Restrictions:** significant restrictions on ownership, realisability, or remittance of income and sale proceeds.
13. **Quoted and unquoted:** the aggregate of quoted investments with their market value, and of unquoted investments.
