# ENGLISH PREDICTED QUESTIONS — ANSWERS

# PART I — SHORT NOTES (5 MARKS)

## Q1.
Akaki Akakievich is a low-ranking government clerk in St. Petersburg. His office is where he spends most of his life. Gogol uses it to show the dull, mechanical life of ordinary clerks.

The office is simple. Akaki's only duty is copying official documents, which he does with care and accuracy. He stays patient and goes on with his work.

For Akaki the office is his whole world. He enjoys copying so much that he copies papers at home. The office also stands for the rigid Russian bureaucracy, where rank matters more than character.

## Q2.
Petrovitch is the tailor who stitches Akaki's new overcoat. He is a minor character, but he changes the course of Akaki's life.

He is experienced, skilled, and proud of his work. He can be stubborn, because he insists on doing the work properly. He believes a new coat is the only honest solution.

Petrovitch then helps Akaki choose good cloth and stitches the overcoat with care. The finished coat is beautiful and becomes Akaki's greatest happiness. Without his decision, the main events of the story would not take place.

## Q3.
After Mudli's mysterious and violent death, Eeri's life is filled with hardship and loneliness. As a widow she has to raise Seena by herself. She loves her son deeply, because he is the only memory of her dead husband.

She works hard to fulfil Seena's wishes. She even buys him a mobile phone, hoping it will make him happy. She later arranges his marriage to Shalini, hoping marriage will steady him, but Seena continues his unhealthy habits.

Eeri suffers in silence while village tales accuse her of immoral relationships and damage her dignity. Her life shows the struggle of a rural widow: poverty, gossip, and an emotional burden, with nobody to support her.

## Q4.
Kirubunkal shows the Indian tradition of storytelling, in which tales, or kathe, are part of the living culture of a community. In the village, tales are not only entertainment. They shape belief, identity, and collective memory.

Elders such as Hirikateyajji carry the tales from one generation to the next. They give life to nature, people, and culture. They explain strange events, moral questions, and social relations.

In Kirubunkal that sacred tradition grows weak. False rumours and sensational stories take the place of meaningful tales. Through the talking tales, the story shows that India's storytelling heritage once guided society, and that it is now threatened by misinformation and the loss of values.

## Q5.
At Masur railway station the station master had the chance to help young Ambedkar and his brothers, but he showed caste prejudice. At first he was polite, because he assumed the children were Brahmins. The moment he learned they were Mahars, an untouchable caste, his manner changed and he treated them with contempt.

He offered them no help, no water and no shelter, and left the small children to wait on the platform. His behaviour shows how deeply untouchability had entered even public service, where an officer's plain duty to help a traveller was set aside because of caste. To the nine-year-old Ambedkar it was an early and painful lesson in social injustice, and it stayed in his mind for the rest of his life.

## Q6.
Ambedkar's father was a disciplined and dutiful man. He served in the army of the East India Company and rose to the rank of Subhedar. After retirement he settled with the family at Satara and worked as a cashier at Koregaon on a famine-relief project.

He valued education and wanted his children to study well. Though he was strict, he cared deeply for his family and their future. His position as a retired army officer gave the family a sense of dignity, but it could not protect them from the harsh caste system, as the children learnt painfully on their journey to meet him.

## Q7.
Nikolai Gogol uses a narrative style that combines realism, humour, satire, irony, and the supernatural.

The story is written in the third person. The narrator knows the characters and describes their thoughts and feelings. Realistic details present Akaki's life and the social conditions of Russia, so the story remains believable.

Satire criticises government offices, bureaucracy, and the pride of officials. Humour is used to expose serious social problems. His greatest happiness becomes the cause of his greatest sorrow.

The story ends with a supernatural element. Akaki's ghost wanders the streets, which creates mystery and stands for justice. The language is simple, and it mixes humour with sadness.

## Q8.
The attack on Mudli is a turning point in Kirubunkal. It brings fear and sadness to the village, and it changes the lives of his family and neighbours.

Seena starts a secret romance with Vimli, the daughter of Chandranna. Chandranna catches them, beats Seena, and drags his daughter away. That same night, Chandranna and his men attack Mudli and beat him to death.

Officially, people say Mudli died of disease during an epidemic. He is buried quickly. The attack shows that even a village can be violent, and it reminds the reader of the value of peace and mutual respect.

## Q9.
The cart driver reflects the caste discrimination of Ambedkar's childhood. At Masur railway station, Ambedkar and the other children wanted a bullock cart to Goregaon. They were ready to pay double the fare, but the drivers believed that carrying untouchables would pollute them.

One driver agreed on a condition. The children had to drive the cart, and he would walk beside it. He told them they would reach soon.

After several hours they reached a toll collector's hut. The driver told them to rest there. His actions show how untouchability made even ordinary travel difficult for children.

## Q10.
Water is a sign of social inequality in On the Way to Goregaon. On the way from Masur, the children were hungry. They had brought food, but they needed water before they could eat it.

Near a stream, the water was dirty and not fit to drink. At the toll collector's hut they hoped for food and water. They had food and still had to stay hungry.

Ambedkar saw that caste discrimination was not limited to social relationships. It reached a basic need such as drinking water. The incident shows the denial of a basic human right.

## Q11.
Sahukar Chandranna is an influential person in the village. As a wealthy man, he plays an important role in the economic and social life of the community. People often approach him for financial help and advice. Through Chandranna, the author shows how wealth can influence village life and relationships. His character also reflects the traditional role of village moneylenders in rural society.

## Q12.
St. Petersburg is famous for its long and freezing winters. Akaki is a poor government clerk who has only an old and torn overcoat. The severe winter forces Akaki to think about buying a new overcoat.

The cold also symbolises the hardness and cruelty of society. After his new overcoat is stolen, Akaki once again has to face the freezing weather. Thus, the cold is not only a natural condition but also a symbol of poverty, loneliness, and human suffering.

## Q13.
The Important Person is a high-ranking government official. When Akaki's new overcoat is stolen, he goes to this official hoping for help. He shouts at Akaki, insults him, and frightens him.

After Akaki dies, his ghost appears and steals the Important Person's overcoat. Thus, Gogol shows that even powerful people can learn the value of kindness.

## Q14.
After seeing Akaki's new overcoat, his colleagues congratulate him warmly. Akaki is shy and not used to attending social gatherings. At the party, he enjoys good food, conversation, and the company of his colleagues.

However, the party lasts until late at night. Thus, the party becomes the turning point in the story.

## Q15.
After months of saving money, Akaki finally receives his new overcoat from Petrovitch. For the first time in his life, Akaki becomes the centre of attention. His colleagues decide that such a wonderful occasion should be celebrated.

Akaki feels happy, proud, and confident. Gogol uses this incident to show that society often judges people by their appearance and possessions rather than by their true character.

## Q16.
After many months of saving money, Akaki finally buys a new overcoat. It is the happiest moment of his life. His colleagues admire the coat and invite him to a party to celebrate.

Akaki attends the party and enjoys the company of his colleagues. It is the first time he feels respected and accepted by society.

## Q17.
Hirikateyajji is the elderly storyteller of the village. She preserves old stories and shares them with the younger generation. She believes that stories protect the culture and identity of the community. She symbolises wisdom, experience, and the rich oral tradition of India. Her famous belief that "The fort of tales must grow" shows her desire to preserve folklore forever.

## Q18.
The village is full of interesting stories passed from one generation to another. These tales include myths, legends, heroic stories, and moral lessons. Every storyteller adds new ideas while keeping the main message alive. The tales entertain people and strengthen community relationships. They also teach honesty, kindness, courage, and respect for elders.

## Q19.
The novella presents village life in a realistic and colourful manner. People live together with strong community relationships. The villagers respect elders and preserve old customs. Young people are influenced by education and city culture. Despite these changes, the village continues to value its traditions and collective identity.

## Q20.
Ambedkar and his brothers were young, innocent, and dependent on others during the journey. They were excited about travelling but soon faced hunger, thirst, and rejection because of their caste. The brothers supported each other, but the painful experiences at Masur taught them harsh lessons about society. Their bond and shared suffering gave Ambedkar the strength to later fight for equality.

## Q21.
The journey to Goregaon exposed Ambedkar to social injustice in its harshest form. He realised that caste discrimination was not just unfair but deeply humiliating. Even as a child, he felt the cruelty of being denied water, kindness, and respect. These incidents planted in him the determination to fight untouchability and later inspired him to work for justice, equality, and dignity for all.

## Q22.
Ambedkar's train journey to Goregaon was both memorable and painful. While the train ride itself was exciting for the children, the real struggle began after they got down at Masur station. They faced rejection, thirst, and humiliation because of their caste. Even though they finally reached Goregaon by cart, the journey left deep scars on young Ambedkar's mind.

# PART I — LONG ANSWERS (10 MARKS)

## Q23.
Nikolai Gogol describes the struggles of ordinary people. The Overcoat is the story of Akaki Akakievich, a poor clerk who lives a lonely and simple life. Through that wish Gogol shows what poverty does to a common man.

Akaki's poverty is clear from the beginning. His salary is very small, and he spends almost all of it on basic needs. There is no luxury and no comfort in his life.

His old overcoat is so worn that it cannot protect him from the freezing winter of St. Petersburg. The tailor tells him that the coat cannot be repaired. Every small coin is saved for the coat.

He never complains. He remains honest and hardworking. Poor people are ignored by society and by government officials.

Gogol uses this life to criticise a society in which hardworking people suffer while the rich enjoy comfort and respect. Akaki's sacrifices, his simple life, and the loss of the coat make the pain of poverty real.

## Q24.
The Overcoat is not only the story of one clerk. It is a picture of class difference in nineteenth-century Russia.

Akaki belongs to the lower class. He earns a small salary and struggles to meet even his basic needs. Society values them for their rank, not for their character.

Akaki's colleagues mock him because he is poor and timid. They ignore his opinions and do not treat him with dignity. Society judges a person by appearance and possessions.

The important official stands for the upper class. He misuses his authority and humiliates Akaki instead of helping him. Akaki loses everything because he has no power and no influence.

Even after Akaki's death, the ghost takes the important official's overcoat. That reversal of power suggests that justice can challenge class inequality. Gogol criticises a society divided by wealth, status, and power, and he asks that every person be given dignity, kindness, and equal treatment.

## Q25.
Ravikumar Neha blends traditional folklore with modern literature. In Kirubunkal the two traditions are the traditional folk culture and the modern way of thinking. He keeps both of them in the story.

The folk tradition appears in oral storytelling, myths, village customs, beliefs, and legends. Hirikateyajji preserves the old stories and passes them to the younger generation. The language is close to everyday speech, like the speech of an elder telling a story in a village.

The modern tradition appears in people who question old beliefs. Seena wants to look modern and fashionable. Modern thinking brings curiosity, scientific ideas, and a change in the way people live.

Neha does not treat one tradition as wholly right and the other as wholly wrong. Folk stories still matter in modern society because they keep cultural identity. Storytelling itself is the bridge between the past and the present.

The combination teaches that a society grows when it respects both tradition and progress. Old stories show people their roots. Modern ideas help them face the future.

## Q26.
On the Way to Goregaon records young Ambedkar's first great shock of untouchability. At Masur railway station the caste system showed itself at its cruellest, because it was aimed at small, helpless children.

When Ambedkar and his brothers got down at Masur, their father had not come to receive them, and no message had reached him. The children turned to the people at the station for help. Even the station master, who could have helped, treated them with contempt once he knew their caste.

The small children had to sit on the platform through the day, rejected only because of the caste they were born into. Their hunger and thirst grew, but nobody would give them even a drop of water. The memory stayed with him and later fired his fight against untouchability.

## Q27.
After the long wait at Masur station, the children's troubles continued on the road to Goregaon. No bullock-cart driver would take them once he knew they were Mahars, even when they offered double the fare. At last one cartman agreed, but only on the cruel condition that the children themselves would drive the cart while he walked beside it, so that he would not be "polluted."

On the way they stopped at a dry river to eat the food they had brought from home, but the water there was too dirty to drink. After his own meal the cartman delayed, which frightened the children; then, forgetting his earlier fear of pollution, he himself sat in the cart and drove. They suspected that the cartman meant to rob them of their gold ornaments, and they cried and begged him to take them safely.

At midnight they reached a toll-collector's hut at the foot of a hill. Hungry and thirsty, they asked for water. The journey showed Ambedkar how even a simple cart ride became an ordeal because of untouchability, and it left a lifelong impression on his mind.

## Q28.
Nikolai Gogol mixes realism with satire and the grotesque. His characters often look unusual, but they stand for deeper truths about human life. Gogol then shows his innocence, loneliness, and humanity.

Akaki lives an unusual life. He has no interest in entertainment, wealth, or social status. This simple nature makes him look strange.

He is completely devoted to his work. Even after office hours he enjoys copying documents at home. He bears the insults in silence and goes on with his work.

The great change in his life is the dream of a new overcoat. His excitement over such a simple thing may seem unusual, but it shows the hardship of a poor man's life. That supernatural change adds another grotesque element.

Although he appears strange, he is kind, honest, hardworking, and innocent. He never harms anyone. Gogol uses this unusual personality to criticise a society that laughs at the weak instead of helping them.

## Q29.
In Kirubunkal, Ravikumar Neha shows the oral tradition of Karnataka. Stories connect generations and preserve culture. Hirikateyajji, the elderly storyteller, is one of the most memorable characters.

Tales travel mainly through oral tradition. Grandparents tell stories to children. They preserve history, culture, values, and collective memory.

Hirikateyajji is an old woman who knows many ancient stories. She is the guardian of traditional knowledge. She reminds them that culture survives only when stories are remembered and shared.

She is the voice of tradition. She connects the past with the present, and her storytelling protects the heritage of the community. Kirubunkal reminds the reader that stories are the true treasure of a society.

## Q30.
Dr. B. R. Ambedkar's journey to Goregaon is one of the most important incidents in On the Way to Goregaon. It became a painful experience that exposed him to untouchability.

During his childhood, Ambedkar and his siblings were living away from their father. Their father, Ramji Sakpal, was working at Koregaon. They were excited because they had not seen him for some time.

They dressed in good clothes and started by train. They carried their luggage and provisions for the journey. He was not at the railway station to receive them.

When they reached Masur railway station, they were alone in an unfamiliar place. They had to arrange transport to Goregaon. The journey exposed young Ambedkar to caste discrimination and to the injustice faced by oppressed people.

## Q31.
After the death of Ambedkar's mother, the household was left in a difficult situation. The children were still very young and could not take care of themselves. Ambedkar's father, though a disciplined and caring man, could not look after them all the time because of his duties.

At this point, Ambedkar's aunt stepped in as a motherly figure. Out of compassion, affection, and a sense of responsibility, she took the charge of raising the young children. She made sure they had food, shelter, and guidance in their early years.

Her sacrifice and care gave the children a sense of belonging and security. It also shows how women in Indian families often take up the responsibility of nurturing children during times of crisis. Ambedkar always remembered her affection and duty towards them.

## Q32.
The title "The Overcoat" is very suitable because the overcoat is the central object around which the whole story revolves. At the beginning of the story, Akaki's old coat is torn and cannot protect him from the severe winter. His tailor tells him that it cannot be repaired. The overcoat becomes a symbol of hope, happiness, and dignity.

Akaki sacrifices his comfort by eating less, walking carefully, and spending very little money. When he finally gets the new overcoat, his life changes. However, this happiness is short-lived. After his death, his ghost is said to wander the streets, stealing overcoats from others.

The overcoat also has a symbolic meaning. Every important event in the story begins or ends with the overcoat.

## Q33.
The main theme of The Overcoat is poverty and social injustice. Akaki is poor and works honestly, but he is ignored and insulted by everyone. His low salary does not allow him to live comfortably. Another important theme is human dignity and respect.

The story also explores the theme of loneliness. Dreams and hope form another major theme. The story also criticises bureaucracy and corruption. Another theme is the lack of compassion.

The supernatural ending introduces the theme of justice. Finally, the story teaches the importance of human values.

## Q34.
Akaki works as a government clerk and earns only a small salary. His old overcoat has become old, torn, and full of patches. One day, he takes the old coat to the tailor, Petrovitch, hoping it can be repaired. After carefully examining it, Petrovitch says the cloth is too weak and that repairing it is impossible.

At first, Akaki is shocked because a new coat is very expensive. He does not have enough money. He begins to live a life of strict economy. Gradually, he saves enough money.

Finally, Akaki and Petrovitch purchase good-quality cloth and prepare a beautiful overcoat. The decision to order the new overcoat marks a turning point in his life.

## Q35.
When Akaki notices that his old overcoat has become worn out, he visits the tailor Petrovitch, hoping that it can be repaired. Petrovitch carefully examines the coat and declares that it is beyond repair. The cloth is too weak, and any attempt to mend it would be useless. Akaki repeatedly requests the tailor to repair the old coat because he cannot afford a new one.

He even offers a small amount of money, hoping Petrovitch will agree. However, Petrovitch firmly refuses. He explains that repairing the old coat would only waste money because it would soon tear again. Gradually, Akaki realises that buying a new overcoat is the only practical solution.

Although Akaki is worried about the cost, he finally accepts the tailor's advice and begins saving money. The tailor's honesty and skill encourage Akaki to order a new overcoat instead of wasting money on repairs.

## Q36.
When Akaki reaches the office wearing his new overcoat, his colleagues immediately notice the change. They gather around him, admire the coat, and congratulate him warmly. For the first time, Akaki receives attention and respect from everyone. People who had ignored him now speak kindly to him.

His colleagues decide to celebrate the occasion. One of the officers invites everyone, including Akaki, to a party in the evening. Akaki feels proud and happy. At the party, he enjoys the company of others, something he had never experienced before.

Late at night, he leaves the party and begins walking home. Thus, the happiest day of Akaki's life ends in great tragedy.

## Q37.
After buying the new overcoat, Akaki is invited to a party by his colleagues. He happily attends the celebration and returns home late at night. While walking through a lonely street, he is suddenly stopped by two unknown thieves. They threaten him, push him to the ground, and steal his new overcoat.

The thieves steal the coat because it is valuable and newly made. In those days, a warm overcoat was expensive and could easily be sold for money or used during the cold winter. After the robbery, Akaki cries for help, but nobody comes to save him. The next day, Akaki reports the theft, but the police do not make serious efforts to recover the coat.

The loss of the overcoat deeply affects Akaki because it represents all his savings, sacrifices, and dreams. The theft is the turning point of the story.

## Q38.
After months of saving money, Akaki finally buys a new overcoat. His colleagues congratulate him and invite him to a celebration. While returning home late at night from the party, Akaki is attacked by thieves. They beat him and steal his new overcoat.

The next morning, Akaki reports the theft to the police, but they show little interest in helping him. Some people advise him to approach an important official who has the authority to help recover the coat. The important official is proud of his high rank. He shouts at Akaki and humiliates him.

The insult affects Akaki both mentally and physically. Because of his illness and disappointment, Akaki dies a few days later.

## Q39.
Soon after Akaki's death, strange rumours spread throughout the city. People claim that a ghost is wandering through the streets at night. The ghost resembles Akaki. It stops people and snatches their overcoats, regardless of their social position.

Many citizens become frightened because the ghost appears unexpectedly and disappears just as quickly. One night, the ghost meets the important official who had insulted Akaki when he asked for help. The ghost forcibly takes away the official's expensive overcoat. After this experience, the official becomes kinder and more respectful towards other people.

Soon afterwards, the ghost disappears, suggesting that Akaki's spirit has finally found peace after receiving justice. The supernatural ending symbolises the victory of justice over cruelty.

## Q40.
A folktale is a traditional story passed from one generation to another by word of mouth. Folktales do not usually have one known author because they are created and preserved by the community. Kirubunkal has many qualities of a folktale. First, the story is based on oral storytelling.

Secondly, the novella contains myths, legends, and village beliefs. Another important feature is the presence of Hirikateyajji, who represents the traditional storyteller. The language of the novella is also simple and close to everyday speech. The characters respect nature, traditions, and community life, which are common themes in folktales.

Although Kirubunkal has many features of a folktale, it is also a modern novella. The author does not simply repeat old stories but interprets them in a modern way.

## Q41.
Seena is deeply obsessed with becoming modern and fashionable. Seena wishes to be different from ordinary village people. Seena follows many methods to look fashionable. He carefully styles his hair and pays great attention to his appearance.

He uses modern accessories to impress others. He tries to speak stylishly and behave confidently in front of others. He often ignores old customs because he thinks they are outdated. Although Seena appears modern from outside, he still belongs to the traditional village community.

The author uses Seena to show how many young people struggle between tradition and modernity. Real development comes through knowledge, good character, and respect for one's cultural roots.

## Q42.
Hirikateyajji is the oldest storyteller in the village. She has spent her life listening to and narrating stories. As she grows old, she notices that the younger generation is becoming more interested in modern life than in traditional tales. They spend less time listening to elders and more time following new ways of living.

She realises that if stories are forgotten, people will also forget their culture, traditions, and ancestors. Therefore, she says, "The fort of tales must grow." The "fort of tales" is a symbol of the rich collection of stories that protect the identity of the village. Hirikateyajji encourages everyone to collect, remember, and share stories.

Her statement also suggests that stories bring people together. Ravikumar Neha uses this idea to remind readers that modern development should not destroy traditional culture.

## Q43.
The narrator believes that a good tale is one that touches the hearts of people and remains alive in their memory. A good tale preserves culture and teaches important values. It entertains people while also giving wisdom and moral guidance. Good stories strengthen relationships within the community and help people understand life.

Such stories are told repeatedly because every generation finds meaning in them. A bad story is quickly forgotten because it neither inspires nor educates people. The narrator believes that stories should connect the past with the present. A tale becomes meaningful when it reflects human experiences, hopes, fears, and dreams.

The narrator values stories that preserve truth, imagination, and cultural identity. Thus, the quality of a tale is judged not by its length but by its impact on people's lives.

## Q44.
Kirubunkal is not just the title of the novella. It is also a powerful symbol that controls and guides the life of the village. Kirubunkal represents the collective memory, traditions, and beliefs of the people. The villagers make many of their decisions based on stories, customs, and traditional values connected with Kirubunkal.

It acts as a moral guide that teaches people honesty, kindness, courage, and respect. Kirubunkal also unites the villagers. The stories related to Kirubunkal influence people's behaviour and shape their understanding of the world. Even when modern ideas enter the village, Kirubunkal continues to preserve cultural continuity.

The author presents Kirubunkal as an invisible force that controls not through fear but through shared traditions and values. It becomes the heart of the village's cultural life.

## Q45.
Ambedkar begins by introducing his family in a simple but respectful manner. He tells us that his family originally came from Dapoli Taluka in Ratnagiri District of the Bombay Presidency. His forefathers, instead of continuing their traditional caste occupations, joined the army of the East India Company.

His father followed this family tradition and rose to become a Subhedar, an officer in the army. Ambedkar's family was not wealthy, but they had a sense of dignity because of their father's service. He had several brothers and sisters.

After the death of his mother, the younger children needed to be taken care of, and Ambedkar's aunt looked after them with affection and a sense of duty. Thus, Ambedkar introduces his family not only by naming their origin and occupation but also by showing their struggles, love, and unity. Through this introduction, we get a clear picture of the hardships and responsibilities faced by the family, especially after the loss of the mother.

## Q46.
Ambedkar's journey to Goregaon exposed him directly to the ideas of ritual purity and caste hierarchy. At Masur station, people refused to touch him or give him water because they believed it would make them impure. Their sense of caste purity was so strong that the suffering of children did not move them.

Later, even the cartman, though kinder than the others, forced the children to sit separately in his cart. This was another reminder that in society's eyes they were "polluting." The cartman was afraid that if they sat with him, he too would be considered impure by others. Thus, throughout the journey, Ambedkar learned how caste rules governed social behaviour in India.

Even the simplest human actions—like drinking water, sitting in a cart, or asking for help—were controlled by the hierarchy of caste.

## Q47.
The painful journey to Goregaon became a turning point in young Ambedkar's life. He learned that society was deeply unjust and that the caste system denied dignity and humanity to millions of people. The humiliation at Masur station and the cart journey taught him that the problem was not just personal but social.

From these bitter experiences, Ambedkar developed a strong sense of determination. He realised that education and courage were the only ways to fight against discrimination. These childhood lessons shaped his future mission to bring equality, justice, and self-respect to the oppressed communities of India.

Later in life, as a great leader, thinker, and the chief architect of the Indian Constitution, Ambedkar often recalled this journey. It gave him the fire to fight against untouchability and to build a society based on liberty, equality, and fraternity.
