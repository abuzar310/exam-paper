## Meaning and collection of data (p. 16)

- **Data** are facts in raw or unorganised form (alphabets, numbers or symbols) that refer to conditions, ideas or objects.
- **Collection of data** is the process of collecting data from surveys or from independent or networked locations via data capture, data entry or data logging. It may be collected for the whole population or for a sample drawn from it.

## Data sources (p. 17)

Data are of two kinds: **primary** and **secondary**.

- **Primary data:** data not previously published, derived from a new or original research study and collected at the source (marketing research, surveys, observation, experimentation).
- Importance of primary data: validity, authenticity, reliability.

### Merits of primary data (p. 18)
1. Degree of accuracy is quite high.
2. It does not require extra caution.
3. It depicts the data in great detail.
4. It often includes definitions of the terms and units used.
5. For some investigations, secondary data are not available.

### Demerits of primary data
1. Collection requires a lot of time.
2. It requires a lot of finance.
3. In some enquiries primary data cannot be collected.
4. It requires a lot of labour.
5. It requires a lot of skill.

## Methods / sources of primary data (pp. 18–20)

1. **Personal investigation / enquiry or direct personal observation.** Information is collected by direct personal interviews. Accurate and reliable; first-hand or original in character. Useful when the scope of the enquiry is small.
   Merits: response is more encouraging, people supply information when approached personally; accurate and additional information may be collected.
   Demerits: expensive and time-consuming; chance of personal prejudice and bias.
2. **Indirect oral interviews.** Information is collected not directly from the source but by interviewing persons closely related to the problem (used to apprehend culprits in theft, murder etc.). The investigator prepares a small list of questions and cross-examines the informants.
   Merits: saves time, relatively less cost; accurate and additional information; vast area covered.
   Demerits: third party may not co-operate; accuracy depends on the integrity of the investigator.
3. **Mailed questionnaire method.** A questionnaire (a document with a set of questions on the enquiry) is mailed to informants with a formal request to answer and return within a specified time. The investigator bears postal charges; a note explains aims, objectives, terms and concepts.
   Questions should be: short and clear; few in number; simple and intelligible; impersonal, non-aggressive; simple-alternative, multiple-choice or open-end type.
4. **Interview schedule method.** Where informants are largely uneducated and non-responsive, enumerators appointed by the investigator meet them with interview schedules, explain the scope and objective, ask the questions and record the answers. Success depends on the sincerity and efficiency of the enumerators.
5. **From local agents.** Local agents or correspondents appointed by the sponsoring authorities, conversant with local language, communication, food habits and traditions, furnish reliable information. Accuracy depends on their honesty and sincerity. Used by government agencies, newspapers, periodicals.
6. **Through telephone.** Researchers get information by telephone; quick and accurate.

## Secondary data (pp. 20–24)

Second-hand information already collected and analysed by someone else for some purpose; not pure in character (e.g. Economic Survey of India). Advantages: ease of access, low cost to acquire, clarification of the research question, may answer the research question, may show difficulties in conducting primary research.
Sources: internal (accounting records, sales force reports, miscellaneous reports, internal experts) and external (databases, associations, government agencies, syndicate services, directories, other published sources, external experts).

## Techniques of data collection (pp. 25–27)

- **Census method:** complete enumeration of every unit of the population. Merits: data from every unit; accurate, representative results; usable for surveys. Demerits: effort, money and time; impossible for an infinite population; expensive.
- **Sampling method:** representative units are selected (the sample) and conclusions drawn for the universe. Principles: statistical regularity, inertia of large numbers.
- Probability sampling: simple random, stratified random, cluster (area), systematic random, multi-stage.
- Non-probability sampling: convenience, judgment (purposive), quota, snowball (reference).

## Classification (pp. 28–29)

**Classification** is the process of dividing the data into different groups or classes which are homogeneous within but heterogeneous between themselves, i.e. arranging data into homogeneous groups or classes according to some common characteristics.

Bases (modes) of classification:
1. **Qualitative base:** by quality or attribute, such as sex, religion, literacy, intelligence.
   (i) Simple classification: only one attribute (population → male, female).
   (ii) Manifold classification: more than one attribute (population → male/female → married/unmarried → literate/illiterate).
2. **Quantitative base:** by measurable characteristics, such as heights, weights, ages, income.
3. **Geographical base:** by regions or location, such as states, provinces, cities, countries (example: sales in Karnataka 500, Tamil Nadu 450, Andhra Pradesh 560, Kerala 350, Rs. lakhs).
4. **Chronological or temporal base:** by time of occurrence, such as years, months, weeks, days (example: sales 2012 3.58, 2013 4.90, 2014 5.36, 2015 5.85, Rs. crores).
