1. Introduction to the HRD System 
What is HRD? 
Human Resource Development (HRD) is a systematic process through which an 
organization develops the knowledge, skills, abilities, attitudes, competencies and 
potential of its employees. 
In simple words: 
HRD means helping employees become more capable today and preparing them to 
handle greater responsibilities tomorrow. 
HRD is therefore much broader than simply conducting training programs. 
For example, suppose a company has a newly promoted manager who is technically 
excellent but cannot manage a team. HRD would not simply send the manager to a 
classroom training program. It might provide: 
• leadership training 
• coaching 
• mentoring 
• feedback 
• performance counselling 
• job rotation 
• challenging assignments 
• career planning 
The objective is to develop the whole capability of the employee. 
Main objectives of HRD 
HRD attempts to: 
1. Improve employee competencies. 
2. Improve individual performance. 
3. Prepare employees for future responsibilities.

## Page 2

4. Develop leadership capabilities. 
5. Increase employee motivation. 
6. Encourage continuous learning. 
7. Improve organizational effectiveness. 
8. Create a positive work environment. 
9. Develop a culture of collaboration and trust. 
10. Align individual development with organizational objectives. 
HRD as a system 
HRD should be viewed as a system rather than a collection of independent 
activities. 
A simple way to remember it is: 
Employee needs → HRD intervention → Learning/development → Improved 
behaviour → Improved performance → Organizational development 
If training is provided but employees never get an opportunity to apply what they 
learned, the HRD system is incomplete. 
 
2. HRD Mechanisms 
Meaning of HRD Mechanisms 
HRD mechanisms are the formal and informal systems, processes and practices used 
by an organization to develop employees. 
They are the tools through which HRD actually operates. 
Important HRD mechanisms include: 
• Performance appraisal 
• Potential appraisal 
• Training and development 
• Feedback and counselling 
• Career planning and development 
• Job rotation 
• Organizational development

• Coaching and mentoring 
• Rewards and recognition 
• Employee welfare 
• Communication 
These mechanisms work together to create a developmental environment. (SLM (Self

A. Performance Appraisal 
Performance appraisal is the systematic assessment of an employee's performance 
against established expectations. 
Traditionally, appraisal was mainly used to answer: 
"How well did the employee perform?" 
Modern HRD asks a second question: 
"How can the employee develop further?" 
HRD importance of performance appraisal 
Performance appraisal helps identify: 
• strengths 
• weaknesses 
• training needs 
• performance gaps 
• career potential 
• future development requirements 
Example 
Suppose an employee is technically competent but has poor presentation skills. 
The appraisal identifies the gap. 
HRD can then provide:

## Page 4

Performance feedback → presentation training → practice → coaching → 
reassessment 
Thus, appraisal becomes a developmental tool, not merely a rating exercise.

4. Training and Development 
Training 
Training generally focuses on improving competencies required for the present job. 
Development 
Development has a broader and longer-term focus and prepares employees for future 
roles and responsibilities. 
Example 
Teaching an employee how to use the company's current accounting software = 
training 
Developing the employee's leadership, strategic thinking and decision-making abilities 
for a future managerial role = development 
Training-development relationship 
Training → present competence 
Development → future capability 
Both are important components of HRD. 
 
5. Feedback and Counselling 
Feedback 
Feedback tells employees how their behaviour or performance compares with 
expected standards. 
Effective feedback should be: 
• specific 
• timely 
• constructive

## Page 6

• understandable 
• focused on behaviour 
• followed by suggestions for improvement 
Poor feedback 
"Your performance is bad." 
This gives the employee little useful information. 
Good feedback 
"Your technical analysis was strong, but your presentation lacked a clear structure. 
Start with the main conclusion and then explain the supporting data." 
This tells the employee what happened and how to improve. 
Counselling 
Employee counselling helps employees understand performance problems, career 
concerns, workplace difficulties and development needs. 
The objective should be improvement rather than punishment.

• employee career goals

7. Job Rotation 
Job rotation means moving an employee periodically between different jobs or 
departments. 
Objectives 
It can: 
• broaden employee experience 
• reduce monotony 
• develop multiple skills 
• improve understanding of the organization 
• prepare employees for management 
• identify employee strengths 
Example 
A management trainee might work in: 
HR → Finance → Marketing → Operations 
The employee develops a broader understanding of the organization.

8. HRD Climate 
This is one of the most important topics in your module. 
Meaning of HRD Climate 
HRD climate refers to the employees' perception of whether their organization provides 
an environment that supports learning, development, openness, trust and growth. 
A good HRD climate makes employees feel: 
"This organization wants me to learn and become better." 
A poor HRD climate makes employees feel: 
"Training is just a formality and mistakes will be punished." 
The HRD climate framework associated with T. V. Rao and E. Abraham emphasizes 
general climate, OCTAPAC culture and HRD mechanisms. (SLM (Self Learning 
Material) for MBA) 
 
9. Components of HRD Climate 
A. General Climate 
General climate refers to the overall organizational environment supporting employee 
development. 
It includes: 
1. Top management support 
Senior managers must demonstrate that employee development is important. 
If top management says: 
"People are our greatest asset"

## Page 9

but never provides time or resources for development, employees will not take HRD 
seriously. 
2. Personnel policies 
Policies should support: 
• fairness 
• development 
• employee welfare 
• learning 
• career growth 
• objective appraisal 
3. Line manager support 
Employees interact with their immediate managers every day. 
Therefore, managers have a major influence on whether employees actually learn and 
develop. 
 
10. OCTAPAC Culture 
This is a very important examination concept. 
OCTAPAC represents seven values: 
O — Openness 
Employees can communicate ideas, concerns and information freely. 
C — Confrontation 
Problems are faced directly rather than ignored. 
Here, confrontation does not mean fighting. 
It means: 
"Let us discuss the real problem and solve it."

## Page 10

T — Trust 
Employees believe that others will act fairly and responsibly. 
A — Autonomy 
Employees have reasonable freedom to make decisions within their responsibilities. 
P — Proactivity 
Employees take initiative instead of waiting for instructions. 
A — Authenticity 
There is consistency between what people say and what they actually do. 
C — Collaboration 
Employees work together rather than operating in unhealthy competition.

11. Organizational Climate vs 
Organizational Culture 
Students often confuse these two.

## Page 11

Climate 
Climate is primarily about how employees perceive the current workplace 
environment. 
Culture 
Culture refers to the deeper values, beliefs, assumptions and norms that guide 
behaviour. 
Think of it this way: 
Climate = how the organization feels 
Culture = why the organization behaves that way 
Climate can change relatively quickly; culture tends to be deeper and slower to change. 
(Wonderslate) 
Example 
Suppose a company currently has an open-door policy. 
Employees feel comfortable speaking to managers. 
That is part of the climate. 
If openness has been a deeply established organizational value for many years and 
employees naturally expect transparent communication, it reflects culture.

13. Employee Behaviour 
Meaning 
Employee behaviour refers to how employees act, think, communicate and respond 
within an organization. 
It includes: 
• work performance 
• attendance 
• cooperation 
• communication 
• decision-making 
• motivation 
• leadership 
• conflict behaviour 
• adaptability 
• organizational citizenship behaviour

## Page 13

HRD is concerned with employee behaviour because development ultimately needs 
to produce useful changes in knowledge, skills, attitudes or behaviour. 
 
14. Model of Employee Behaviour 
A useful way to understand employee behaviour is: 
Individual factors + Environment + Situation → Behaviour → Performance/Outcomes 
For example: 
An employee may have excellent technical ability but perform poorly because: 
• the manager does not provide feedback, 
• resources are unavailable, 
• workload is excessive, 
• goals are unclear, 
• organizational policies are unfair. 
Therefore, employee behaviour cannot always be explained simply by saying: 
"The employee is good" or "The employee is bad." 
Behaviour is influenced by the interaction between the person and environment. 
Social cognitive theory similarly views behaviour as a dynamic interaction among the 
person, environment and behaviour. (Sage Journals) 
 
15. Internal Factors Influencing 
Employee Behaviour 
Internal factors originate largely within the individual. 
1. Personality 
Personality influences how employees respond to situations.

• an extroverted employee may enjoy social interaction 
• a highly conscientious employee may be very organized 
• an emotionally stable employee may remain calm under pressure 
2. Perception 
Perception is how an employee interprets information and situations.

3. Attitude 
Attitude represents an employee's positive or negative orientation toward something. 
Examples: 
• attitude toward work 
• attitude toward management 
• attitude toward change 
4. Motivation 
Motivation determines the direction, intensity and persistence of behaviour. 
A motivated employee is more likely to invest effort toward goals. 
5. Ability 
Ability refers to the employee's capacity to perform a task. 
It includes: 
• intellectual ability 
• physical ability 
• technical ability 
• interpersonal ability

6. Values

• honesty 
• achievement

• fairness

7. Learning 
Past experiences influence future behaviour. 
An employee who has learned that mistakes lead to constructive feedback may be 
more willing to experiment.

17. Learning and HRD 
Now we come to the second major part of the module. 
What is Learning? 
Learning is a relatively lasting change in knowledge, skills, attitudes or behaviour 
resulting from experience, practice, instruction or interaction with the 
environment. 
In HRD, learning is important because training is useful only when employees actually 
learn and transfer that learning to their jobs.

## Page 17

Modern workplace-learning research emphasizes acquisition and retention of 
knowledge, skills and affect, followed by application when needed. (Annual Reviews) 
 
18. Learning Principles 
These principles help HR professionals design effective learning programs. 
Principle 1: Motivation 
People learn better when they have a reason to learn. 
Example 
An employee is more motivated to learn Excel when they understand: 
"This skill will help me prepare reports faster and qualify for higher responsibilities." 
 
Principle 2: Readiness 
A person should be mentally and physically ready to learn. 
If an employee is exhausted, distracted or completely uninterested, learning becomes 
difficult. 
 
Principle 3: Practice 
Practice strengthens learning. 
Learning + repeated practice → stronger skill 
For example, leadership cannot be learned effectively only by reading a textbook. The 
employee needs opportunities to practice leadership.

## Page 18

Principle 4: Feedback 
Learners need information about their performance. 
Feedback helps answer: 
• What did I do correctly? 
• What did I do incorrectly? 
• How can I improve? 
 
Principle 5: Reinforcement 
Behaviours that receive appropriate positive consequences are more likely to be 
repeated. 
For example: 
An employee uses a new problem-solving technique effectively → manager recognizes 
the effort → employee is encouraged to continue using the technique. 
 
Principle 6: Active Participation 
People generally learn better when they actively participate rather than simply listen. 
Therefore: 
Case study + discussion + practice 
is often more useful than: 
two-hour lecture only 
 
Principle 7: Meaningfulness 
Learning should be connected to the learner's existing knowledge and real work. 
Employees learn more effectively when they can answer:

## Page 19

"Why do I need to know this?" 
 
Principle 8: Transfer 
Learning must be transferred from the training environment to the workplace. 
This is one of the biggest challenges in HRD. 
Example 
An employee attends communication training. 
If the employee never applies the techniques at work, the organization gets little 
benefit. 
Therefore: 
Training → Practice → Workplace application → Performance improvement 
 
19. Maximizing Learning 
Maximizing learning means creating conditions under which employees can learn 
efficiently, deeply and consistently. 
Important factors include: 
1. Clear learning objectives 
Learners should know exactly what they are expected to achieve. 
2. Appropriate difficulty 
Training should neither be extremely easy nor impossibly difficult. 
3. Relevant content 
Content should relate to actual job requirements.

## Page 20

4. Active practice 
Learners should perform tasks rather than merely hear about them. 
5. Feedback 
Feedback allows correction. 
6. Repetition 
Important skills need repeated practice. 
7. Application 
Learners should receive opportunities to use their knowledge. 
8. Supportive environment 
Managers and coworkers should support the learning process. 
9. Follow-up 
Learning should be reinforced after formal training. 
10. Evaluation 
Organizations should determine whether learning actually improved performance. 
Instructional psychology specifically focuses on designing learning conditions to 
maximize acquisition of competence, including identifying goals, understanding the 
learner's starting point, selecting appropriate instructional conditions, and monitoring 
progress. (Ebook App)

Process 
This is another important concept. 
Not everyone learns in exactly the same way or at the same speed.

21. Learning Strategies 
A learning strategy is a deliberate method used to improve learning. 
Important strategies include: 
1. Rehearsal 
Repeating information or skills. 
Example:

## Page 22

Practicing a presentation several times. 
2. Elaboration 
Connecting new information with existing knowledge. 
Example: 
Connecting a new HR theory to a real company example. 
3. Organization 
Arranging information into meaningful structures. 
Example: 
Using headings, diagrams and concept maps. 
4. Practice 
Repeatedly performing the desired skill. 
5. Self-regulation 
The learner monitors and controls their own learning. 
This includes: 
Set goal → study → monitor progress → identify weakness → change strategy 
6. Collaborative learning 
Employees learn through interaction with others. 
Examples: 
• group discussion 
• peer learning 
• team projects 
• mentoring

22. Learning Styles 
Learning-style theories classify learners according to preferred ways of learning. 
Common classifications discuss preferences such as: 
• visual 
• auditory 
• reading/writing 
• kinesthetic 
However, there is an important modern qualification: 
Do not assume that teaching must always be matched to a person's supposed 
fixed "learning style." 
Contemporary workplace-learning research cautions against the simplistic idea that 
people necessarily learn better when instruction is matched to a self-identified learning 
style. It emphasizes evidence-based instructional principles instead. (Annual Reviews) 
What should an HR professional do instead? 
Use multiple effective instructional methods: 
• explanation 
• demonstration 
• examples 
• practice 
• discussion 
• simulation 
• feedback 
• reflection 
The goal should be effective learning, not merely labeling employees as "visual 
learners" or "auditory learners."

23. Instructional Psychology 
Meaning 
Instructional psychology studies how learning environments and instructional 
methods can be designed to produce effective learning. 
Traditional psychology often asks: 
"How does learning happen?" 
Instructional psychology also asks: 
"What should we do to help this person learn?" 
This distinction is important. 
A classic instructional approach emphasizes four steps: 
1. Define the learning goal. 
2. Determine the learner's current knowledge/ability. 
3. Select appropriate instructional conditions. 
4. Assess and monitor learning progress. (Ebook App) 
Application in HRD 
Suppose an organization wants employees to learn customer-service skills. 
Step 1 — Goal 
Employees should be able to handle difficult customers professionally. 
Step 2 — Starting point 
Assess current communication skills. 
Step 3 — Instruction 
Use: 
• demonstrations 
• role plays 
• case studies

## Page 25

• simulations 
Step 4 — Assessment 
Observe employees handling simulated customer complaints. 
This is instructional psychology applied to HRD. 
 
24. Cognitive Psychology 
Meaning 
Cognitive psychology studies mental processes involved in: 
• attention 
• perception 
• memory 
• thinking 
• reasoning 
• problem-solving 
• decision-making 
• learning 
It views humans as active processors of information. 
In other words: 
Employees do not simply receive information; they interpret, organize, store and 
retrieve it. 
Cognitive psychology has significantly influenced modern instructional design and 
HRD. (Ebook App)

26. Cognitive Load 
Human mental processing capacity is limited. 
If a trainer gives learners too much complicated information simultaneously, the 
learner can become cognitively overloaded. 
Example 
Imagine a trainer showing: 
• 30 slides 
• 15 complicated graphs 
• 10 new concepts 
• technical terminology 
• a long case study 
all within 20 minutes. 
The problem may not be that the learner is unintelligent. 
The problem may be excessive cognitive load. 
HRD implication 
Training should: 
• break complex material into manageable units 
• use clear explanations 
• provide examples 
• avoid unnecessary information 
• allow practice 
• connect new concepts with existing knowledge

2. Experiential learning

3. Simulation 
Employees practice realistic situations in a safe environment. 
4. Microlearning 
Learning content is divided into short, focused units.

A 7-minute module teaching one Excel function. 
5. E-learning 
Technology is used to provide learning digitally. 
6. Adaptive learning

7. Social learning 
Employees learn by observing and interacting with others.

8. Self-directed learning 
Employees increasingly take responsibility for identifying their own learning needs and 
pursuing development.

30. Exam-Ready Short Notes 
HRD Mechanisms 
HRD mechanisms are systems and practices used to develop employee capabilities. 
Major mechanisms include performance appraisal, potential appraisal, training and 
development, feedback and counselling, career development, job rotation, 
organizational development and rewards. 
HRD Climate 
HRD climate is the employees' perception of the extent to which an organization 
supports employee development. It is influenced by general organizational climate, 
OCTAPAC culture and HRD mechanisms. 
OCTAPAC 
OCTAPAC represents Openness, Confrontation, Trust, Autonomy, Proactivity, 
Authenticity and Collaboration. These values encourage a developmental 
organizational environment. 
Employee Behaviour 
Employee behaviour refers to how employees think, feel and act within an organization. 
It is influenced by individual factors such as personality, perception, motivation, ability 
and values, as well as external factors such as leadership, culture, rewards, job design 
and organizational policies.

## Page 33

Learning 
Learning is a relatively lasting change in knowledge, skills, attitudes or behaviour 
resulting from experience, practice or instruction. 
Instructional Psychology 
Instructional psychology focuses on designing learning conditions that facilitate the 
acquisition of competence. It emphasizes learning objectives, learner characteristics, 
instructional conditions and assessment. 
Cognitive Psychology 
Cognitive psychology examines mental processes such as attention, memory, thinking, 
reasoning and problem-solving and helps HRD professionals design more effective 
learning experiences.
