Hours prescribed in syllabus: 12
1. Introduction to Training and Development
Meaning of Training
Training is a planned process of teaching employees the knowledge, skills and work-related behaviour needed to perform
their present jobs effectively. It focuses mainly on improving the employee’s ability to do a particular job.
Example: A newly appointed cashier is trained to use billing software, handle cash, issue receipts and follow
cash-control procedures.
Meaning of Development
Development is a broader and longer-term process that prepares employees for future responsibilities, higher positions
and changing organisational needs. It improves an individual’s overall abilities, judgement, confidence and professional
growth.
Example: A company provides leadership, decision-making and strategic-planning programmes to an employee who
may become a department manager.

2. Scope of Training
The scope of training refers to the areas and activities in which employees may need learning and improvement. Training
is not limited to newly recruited employees; it may be required at every level and throughout an employee’s career.
• Induction training: introducing new employees to the organisation, rules, people and workplace.
• Job or technical training: teaching the knowledge and practical skills required for a particular job.
• Safety training: teaching safe work practices and emergency procedures.
• Soft-skills training: improving communication, teamwork, time management and interpersonal skills.
• Supervisory and managerial training: developing planning, delegation, leadership and decision-making.
• Quality and customer-service training: improving service standards and product quality.
• Digital and technology training: teaching employees to use new software, equipment and digital systems.
• Compliance and ethics training: explaining workplace rules, professional conduct and applicable legal requirements.
3. Training Need Analysis (TNA)
Meaning
Training Need Analysis is the systematic process of identifying the gap between the knowledge or skills employees
currently have and the knowledge or skills they need to perform their jobs effectively. It helps an organisation decide who
needs training, what training is required and why.
Example: A retail company notices that customers are complaining because sales employees cannot explain product
features. TNA identifies product knowledge and communication as training needs.
Objectives of TNA
• Identify the actual training gaps instead of arranging training without a clear purpose.
• Determine which employees or departments need training.
• Select suitable training content and methods.
• Use the training budget effectively.
• Improve employee performance and organisational results.
• Provide a basis for evaluating training effectiveness.

Steps in TNA
• Identify the organisational problem or performance objective.
• Collect information through observation, interviews, questionnaires, performance reports and discussions.
• Study the organisation, job requirements and individual employees.
• Compare existing skills with the skills required.
• Identify the causes of the gap and confirm whether training can solve it.
• Set training priorities and objectives.
• Recommend the training content, method, participants and schedule.
Methods Used to Identify Training Needs
• Observation of employees while they perform their jobs.
• Interviews with employees, supervisors and managers.
• Questionnaires and employee surveys.
• Performance appraisal reports.
• Customer complaints and quality reports.
• Skill tests, work samples and job-knowledge tests.
• Changes in technology, law, products or organisational strategy.
Important point: Not every performance problem can be solved by training. A lack of equipment, unclear instructions,
unrealistic targets or poor working conditions may require a different solution.
4. Types of Training: Internal and External
Internal Training
Internal training is organised and conducted within the organisation, using its own managers, supervisors, trainers,
facilities or work systems.
Example: A senior accountant trains newly recruited accounts assistants in the company’s accounting procedures.
• Advantages: training can be designed around the organisation’s actual work; it may be less expensive; employees learn
familiar procedures; schedules can be flexible.
• Limitations: internal trainers may lack specialist knowledge; employees may learn only existing practices; work may be
interrupted; facilities may be limited.
External Training
External training is provided by an outside institution, professional trainer, consultancy, college, industry association or
specialist training centre.
Example: A company sends its HR executives to a professional institute for a programme on labour-law updates.
• Advantages: access to specialist trainers and updated knowledge; exposure to different organisations and ideas; useful
for specialised subjects.
• Limitations: fees and travel may be high; content may not fully match the organisation’s needs; employees may need help
applying what they learned to their jobs.

5. Outbound Training
Meaning
Outbound training is a learning method in which employees participate in structured activities outside the usual
workplace, often in an outdoor or unfamiliar setting. Activities are designed to develop teamwork, leadership,
communication, trust, planning and problem-solving.
Example: A group of employees takes part in a supervised team challenge that requires them to plan, communicate and
complete a task together.
Objectives
• Develop teamwork and cooperation.
• Improve leadership and decision-making.
• Build trust among team members.
• Improve communication and listening.
• Develop problem-solving and planning skills.
• Build confidence and adaptability.
Common Outbound Training Activities
• Team-building games and group challenges.
• Obstacle-course activities conducted with appropriate safety arrangements.
• Problem-solving tasks and treasure hunts.
• Group planning and outdoor projects.
• Reflection and discussion sessions after activities.
Advantages and Limitations
• Advantages: experiential learning, active participation, stronger relationships and an opportunity to observe team
behaviour.
• Limitations: weather, safety, accessibility and cost may be concerns; activities must be linked to learning objectives and
followed by reflection.
Key point: Outbound training is not simply an outing or recreational event. It must have planned learning objectives,
suitable supervision and a discussion connecting the activity to workplace behaviour.
6. Attitudinal Training
Meaning
Attitudinal training aims to develop positive work-related attitudes, values and behaviour. It helps employees develop a
constructive approach towards work, customers, colleagues, change and organisational responsibilities.
Example: Employees who respond rudely to customer complaints may attend a programme on empathy, patience,
service attitude and professional communication.

Objectives
• Develop a positive attitude towards work.
• Encourage discipline, responsibility and punctuality.
• Improve customer orientation and service behaviour.
• Promote respect, cooperation and teamwork.

Importance

training alone cannot create a positive culture if organisational policies and management behaviour contradict the values
being taught.
7. Principles Involved in Selection of a Training Method
A training method should be selected according to the learning objective, nature of the job, participants and available
resources. One method is not suitable for every training need.
• Training objectives: select a method that helps achieve the specific learning outcome.
• Nature of the job: practical jobs may require demonstration and hands-on practice; conceptual subjects may need
discussion or case studies.
• Participants’ level: consider age, education, experience, language and existing knowledge.
• Number of trainees: a lecture may suit a large group, while coaching may suit an individual or small group.
• Cost and budget: consider trainer fees, materials, travel, equipment and time away from work.
• Time available: choose a method that fits the training schedule without reducing learning quality.
• Trainer competence: ensure the trainer can use the selected method effectively.
• Learning by doing: provide opportunities to practise and receive feedback wherever possible.
• Transfer to the job: choose methods that help employees apply learning at work.
• Evaluation: select a method whose results can be assessed against the training objectives.
• Safety and accessibility: ensure activities are safe, inclusive and suitable for participants.
8. Techniques of Training at Different Levels
Training techniques differ according to the responsibilities and experience of employees. Operational employees need
practical job skills, supervisors need people-management skills, and managers need wider planning and decision-making
abilities.
A. Training for Workers / Operational Employees
• On-the-job training: learning while performing the actual job under guidance.
• Demonstration: trainer shows the correct way to operate equipment or perform a task.

9. Training Effectiveness
Meaning
Training effectiveness refers to the extent to which a training programme achieves its objectives and improves
participants’ knowledge, skills, behaviour or job performance.
Why Evaluate Training Effectiveness?
• To check whether learning objectives were achieved.
• To find out whether employees can apply new skills at work.
• To identify strengths and weaknesses in the training programme.
• To decide whether the programme should be continued, modified or replaced.
• To understand whether training contributes to organisational results.

MODULE 5: RECENT TRENDS IN HRD
Hours prescribed in syllabus: 12
This module explains emerging practices and changing priorities in Human Resource Development. Organisations
increasingly focus on professional trainers, research, employee awareness, industrial relations, effective use of HRD,
technology, coaching, mentoring and competency-based development.
1. Introduction to Recent Trends in HRD
Recent trends in HRD are new approaches, practices and developments used by organisations to improve employee
capabilities and organisational performance. HRD is no longer limited to classroom training. It also includes continuous
learning, research, technology-enabled learning, coaching, mentoring, competency development and strategic employee
engagement.
Major Trends
• Continuous learning and reskilling to keep employees updated.
• Digital learning platforms and technology-supported training.
• Coaching and mentoring for individual development.
• Competency-based HRD and competency mapping.
• Greater use of research and data to plan HRD activities.
• Greater attention to employee engagement, workplace relationships and organisational culture.
• Measurement of training outcomes and business impact.
2. Training for Trainers and HRD Professionals
Meaning
Training for trainers means developing the knowledge and skills of people who deliver training. HRD professionals are
employees who plan, coordinate, implement and evaluate human resource development activities. They need
professional capabilities to understand employee needs and design effective learning programmes.
Why Trainers Need Training
• To improve subject knowledge and training skills.
• To learn how adults learn and how to involve participants.
• To use suitable teaching methods, activities and learning materials.
• To manage groups and handle questions effectively.
• To evaluate whether participants have learned.
• To use digital tools and blended learning methods.

3. Promoting Research in HRD
Meaning
Research in HRD is the systematic collection and analysis of information to understand employee development,
workplace learning, organisational behaviour and the effectiveness of HRD practices. Research helps organisations
make decisions based on evidence rather than assumptions.
Objectives of HRD Research
• Identify employee learning and development needs.
• Understand factors affecting employee performance and motivation.
• Evaluate the effectiveness of training programmes.
• Study organisational climate, culture and employee behaviour.
• Identify new HRD practices and emerging skill requirements.
• Improve HRD policies and organisational decision-making.
Areas of Research in HRD
• Training needs and learning preferences.
• Employee engagement, motivation and job satisfaction.
• Leadership development and succession planning.
• Workplace learning and knowledge sharing.
• Impact of technology and digital learning.
• Training effectiveness and return on investment.
• Competency requirements for present and future jobs.
Steps in Conducting HRD Research
• Identify the research problem or question.
• Review relevant information and previous studies.
• Set research objectives.
• Select a suitable method, such as survey, interview, observation or case study.
• Collect information ethically and accurately.
• Analyse the information and identify patterns.
• Draw conclusions and make practical recommendations.
• Share findings and use them to improve HRD practices.
Example: An organisation surveys employees after a training programme, interviews supervisors and compares error
rates before and after training to understand whether the programme helped.

4. Creating Awareness and Commitment to HRD
Meaning
Creating awareness of HRD means helping employees and managers understand the purpose, benefits and
opportunities of employee development. Creating commitment means encouraging them to actively support and
participate in HRD activities.
Why Awareness and Commitment are Important
• Employees may not participate if they do not understand the purpose of training.
• Managers must provide time, support and resources for learning.
• Employees are more likely to apply learning when supervisors encourage them.
• An HRD culture supports continuous improvement and organisational growth.
Methods of Creating Awareness and Commitment
• Communicate HRD policies, objectives and opportunities clearly.
• Conduct orientation sessions and awareness programmes.
• Explain how learning supports employees’ careers and organisational goals.
• Obtain visible support from senior management.
• Encourage managers to discuss development during performance reviews.
• Recognise employees who apply new learning and share knowledge.
• Invite employees to give feedback on training needs and programmes.
Example: Before introducing a digital-skills programme, management explains why the new system is needed, how the
training will help employees and what support will be available.
5. Industrial Relations and HRD
Meaning of Industrial Relations
Industrial relations refer to the relationship between employers, employees and their representatives, including trade
unions, in matters relating to employment, working conditions, communication and workplace cooperation.
Relationship Between Industrial Relations and HRD
HRD can support healthy industrial relations by improving communication, employee skills, supervisor behaviour, trust
and participation. Good industrial relations can also create a stable environment in which employees are more willing to
learn and contribute.
Role of HRD in Improving Industrial Relations
• Communication training helps managers and employees discuss issues clearly.
• Supervisory development helps managers treat employees fairly and handle grievances.
• Team-building activities improve cooperation between departments and groups.
• Employee education helps workers understand policies, safety and organisational changes.
• Conflict-management training supports constructive resolution of disagreements.
• Participation in problem-solving can build trust and shared responsibility.
Benefits
• Improves understanding between management and employees.
• Can reduce misunderstandings and workplace conflict.
• Encourages cooperation and employee participation.
• Supports productivity and workplace stability.

6. Utilization of HRD Efforts
Meaning
Utilisation of HRD efforts means making proper use of the knowledge, skills, abilities and competencies developed
through training and other HRD activities. Training creates value only when employees get opportunities to apply what
they have learned.
Ways to Utilise HRD Efforts
• Assign employees duties that allow them to use their newly acquired skills.
• Provide the tools, technology and authority needed to apply learning.
• Encourage supervisors to support and monitor workplace application.
• Use job rotation and project assignments to broaden experience.
• Create opportunities for employees to share knowledge with colleagues.
• Include relevant competencies in performance discussions and career planning.
• Review whether the training is being applied and remove barriers.
Barriers to Utilisation
• Lack of equipment, time or management support.
• Workplace procedures that do not allow employees to use new methods.
• Fear of making mistakes or resistance to change.
• Training content that is not relevant to actual job requirements.
• No follow-up, feedback or opportunity to practise.
Example: An employee trained in advanced spreadsheet skills should be given suitable work assignments and access to
the software so that the new skills can be used.
7. Future of HRD
The future of HRD will be influenced by changes in technology, business needs, employee expectations and the nature of
work. Organisations will need to support continuous learning and prepare employees for new roles and skills.
Likely Developments and Priorities
• Continuous reskilling and upskilling as job requirements change.
• Greater use of online, mobile and blended learning.
• Personalised learning based on an employee’s role, skill gaps and career goals.
• Use of data and analytics to identify learning needs and evaluate results.
• Greater focus on digital, analytical, communication and problem-solving skills.
• Learning that supports innovation, adaptability and collaboration.
• More attention to employee wellbeing, inclusion and sustainable work practices.
• Closer alignment between HRD strategy and organisational strategy.
Reskilling means learning new skills for a different or changing role. Upskilling means improving or extending skills
needed in the current or a closely related role.

employees. It helps organisations understand different training systems, workplace learning practices, leadership

10. Coaching and Mentoring
A. Coaching – Meaning
Coaching is a structured process in which a coach helps an employee improve specific skills, solve work-related
challenges or achieve defined performance goals through questions, guidance, practice and feedback.
Example: A sales manager coaches a sales executive on asking better questions, explaining product benefits and
responding to customer objections.
Features and Benefits of Coaching
• Usually focuses on specific performance goals or skills.
• May be short-term or ongoing, depending on the need.
• Encourages self-reflection and practical action.
• Provides feedback and helps track progress.
• Can improve confidence, performance and problem-solving.
B. Mentoring – Meaning
Mentoring is a developmental relationship in which a more experienced person (mentor) provides guidance, advice,
support and knowledge to a less experienced person (mentee) to support wider professional and career development.
Example: An experienced finance manager guides a newly appointed finance executive about career development,
professional conduct and handling responsibilities.
Features and Benefits of Mentoring
• Often focuses on long-term professional and career development.
• Uses the mentor’s experience and knowledge.
• Can support confidence, career planning and organisational understanding.
• Encourages knowledge sharing between experienced and less experienced employees.

11. Competency Framework of HRD
Meaning of Competency
A competency is a combination of knowledge, skills, abilities and behaviours that enables a person to perform a job or
role effectively. Competencies describe not only what a person knows, but also how the person applies that knowledge
and behaves at work.
Example: A successful HR executive may need knowledge of recruitment, interviewing skills, communication ability,
confidentiality and fair judgement.
Meaning of Competency Framework
A competency framework is an organised structure that identifies the competencies required for different jobs, roles or
levels in an organisation. It provides a common reference for recruitment, training, performance management, promotion
and career development.

12. Understanding the Competency Mapping Framework
Meaning of Competency Mapping
Competency mapping is the process of identifying the competencies required for a particular job and comparing them
with the competencies an employee currently possesses. It helps identify the gap between required and existing
capabilities.
Example: A job requires advanced Excel, report writing and communication. An employee has good communication but
only basic Excel skills. The mapping process identifies advanced Excel as a development need.

Steps in Competency Mapping
• Identify the job or role to be studied.
• Understand the duties, responsibilities and expected results of the role.
• Identify the knowledge, skills, abilities and behaviours required.
• Define each competency clearly and describe expected proficiency levels.
• Assess the employee using interviews, tests, observation, work samples or performance evidence.
• Compare the employee’s current level with the required level.
• Identify competency gaps and prioritise development needs.
• Prepare an individual development plan with suitable training, coaching, mentoring or work experience.
• Review progress and update the competency map when job requirements change.

14. Balanced Scorecard
Meaning
The Balanced Scorecard is a strategic performance management framework that helps an organisation translate its
strategy into objectives and measures. It examines performance from more than one perspective rather than focusing
only on financial results. In HRD, it can help connect employee learning and development with internal processes,
customer outcomes and financial performance.

Importance in HRD
• Connects employee learning with organisational strategy.

• Helps managers monitor progress through clear indicators.

Example: A bank may train employees in digital banking. Learning and growth measures may track digital skills;
internal-process measures may track transaction errors; customer measures may track satisfaction; and financial
measures may track operating efficiency.
15. Appreciative Inquiry
Meaning
Appreciative Inquiry is an approach to organisational development that focuses on identifying and building on an
organisation’s strengths, successful experiences and positive possibilities. Instead of concentrating only on what is
wrong, it asks what is working well and how those strengths can be expanded.
Main Principles
• Focus on strengths and successful practices.
• Encourage participation and shared discussion.
• Use positive questions to discover opportunities.
• Create a shared picture of a desirable future.
• Turn ideas into practical action and continuous improvement.

• Encourages employee participation and positive communication.
