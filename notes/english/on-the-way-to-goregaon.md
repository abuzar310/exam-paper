3. ON THE WAY TO GOREGAON
SUMMARY OF THE LESSON 
Family Background
1. The narrator’s family was originally from Dapoli, Ratnagiri (Bombay Presidency).
2. His forefathers had left their traditional occupation and served in the East India Company’s Army.
3. His father also served in the army and retired as a Subhedar.
4. After retirement, the family moved to Satara around 1901.
Situation at Satara
5. The narrator’s mother had passed away.
6. His father worked as a cashier at Koregaon (Satara District) on a famine relief project.
7. The children (narrator, his brother, and nephews) lived with their handicapped aunt, who could not cook or manage the house.
8. The children cooked for themselves—mostly pulav (rice with meat), as they could not make bread.
The Journey Begins
9. During summer vacation, the father invited them to Koregaon.
10. The children were excited to travel by train for the first time.
11. They bought new clothes, caps, shoes, and dhoties for the trip.
12. They hired a tonga (horse-cart) to go 10 miles to the railway station.
13. They were given pocket money and enjoyed lemonade at the station.
14. They boarded the train and got down at Masur station in the evening.
At Masur Station:
15. Nobody came to receive them, though their father had promised to send someone.
16. The station-master was kind at first and assumed they were Brahmin children.
17. When the narrator revealed they were Mahars (untouchables), his attitude changed suddenly.
18. No bullock-cart driver agreed to take them because they were untouchables, even when offered double fare.
19. Finally, one cartman agreed on the condition that the children themselves would drive the cart, while he walked beside it.
On the Way to Koregaon
20. They stopped at a dry river to eat food brought from home but could not drink the dirty water.
21. The cartman delayed returning from his meal, making them anxious.
22. After some time, the cartman himself sat in the cart, forgetting his earlier fear of “pollution.”
23. Darkness fell, and the children became frightened.
24. They suspected the cartman of tricking them for their gold ornaments.
25. They cried and begged him to take them safely.
26. At midnight, they reached a toll-collector’s hut at the foot of a hill.
The Night Struggle
27. Hungry and thirsty, they asked for water.
28. On the cartman’s advice, they pretended to be Muslims, but the toll-collector still refused to give water.
29. They had food but could not eat without water.
30. They slept hungry in the cart, keeping watch in turns out of fear.
Reaching Koregaon
31. Next morning, they continued and reached Koregaon by 11 a.m.
32. Their father was surprised, saying he had never received their letter.
33. It was later found that his servant had hidden the letter.
Realization about Untouchability
34. At school, the narrator already faced discrimination:
Could not sit with other children.
Had to bring his own gunny cloth to sit on.
Could not touch the school tap; needed the peon to open it. (“No peon, no water”).
35. At home:
Sisters washed clothes because washermen refused.
Sisters cut hair because barbers refused.
36. The journey incident, however, gave the narrator his first big shock about untouchability.
37. It made him think deeply about the cruelty and injustice of caste discrimination.
👉 This event left a lifelong impression on him at the age of nine.
Answer the following questions:
How does boy Ambedkar introduce all the members of his father’s family?
Ambedkar begins by introducing his family in a simple but respectful manner. He tells us that his family originally came from Dapoli Taluka in Ratnagiri District of the Bombay Presidency. His forefathers, instead of continuing their traditional caste occupations, joined the army of the East India Company. His father followed this family tradition and rose to become a Subhedar, an officer in the army. After retirement, Ambedkar’s father settled with his family.
Ambedkar’s family was not wealthy, but they had a sense of dignity because of their father’s service. He had several brothers and sisters. After the death of his mother, the younger children needed to be taken care of, and Ambedkar’s aunt looked after them with affection and a sense of duty.
Thus, Ambedkar introduces his family not only by naming their origin and occupation but also by showing their struggles, love, and unity. Through this introduction, we get a clear picture of the hardships and responsibilities faced by the family, especially after the loss of the mother.

What made Ambedkar’s aunt look after the young siblings of the family?
After the death of Ambedkar’s mother, the household was left in a difficult situation. The children were still very young and could not take care of themselves. Ambedkar’s father, though a disciplined and caring man, could not look after them all the time because of his duties.
At this point, Ambedkar’s aunt stepped in as a motherly figure. Out of compassion, affection, and a sense of responsibility, she took the charge of raising the young children. She made sure they had food, shelter, and guidance in their early years.
Her sacrifice and care gave the children a sense of belonging and security. It also shows how women in Indian families often take up the responsibility of nurturing children during times of crisis. Ambedkar always remembered her affection and duty towards them.

Trace the circumstances leading to Ambedkar’s train journey to Goregaon.
The train journey to Goregaon happened under special circumstances. Ambedkar and his brothers were studying in Satara after the death of their mother. Their father, who had retired from the army, was staying at Goregaon in another part of the Bombay Presidency.
During the school holidays, Ambedkar’s father wanted his children to come and stay with him at Goregaon. Since the children were very young and had never travelled alone before, arrangements were made for them to go by train to Masur station, from where their father was supposed to meet them and take them to Goregaon.
However, when they reached Masur, their father had not arrived. This unexpected situation became the beginning of a very painful and unforgettable experience for young Ambedkar and his siblings.

Briefly narrate young Ambedkar’s experiences at Masur railway station.
When Ambedkar and his brothers reached Masur railway station, they were excited at first. But soon they realized their father had not come to receive them. They were small children and had no money to arrange a cart for themselves. They waited for a long time, but nobody from their family arrived.
Ambedkar tried to get help from people at the station. However, the moment people came to know that the children were Mahar by caste, their attitudes changed completely. They refused to offer water or shelter, fearing they would lose their caste purity if they touched the children. The stationmaster too treated them with contempt.
The young Ambedkar felt deep pain and humiliation at such treatment. Hunger, thirst, and loneliness troubled the children as they sat on the platform the whole day, rejected by society only because of their caste. This became one of the earliest and sharpest memories of social injustice in his life.

Briefly narrate young Ambedkar’s journey to Goregaon by cart from Masur.
After waiting long hours at Masur, the children were in despair. Finally, one Muslim cartman, who was kinder than the others, agreed to take them to Goregaon. But even here, caste played a cruel role. The cartman told the children that since they were Mahars, he could not allow them to sit inside the cart. Instead, they had to sit on sacks spread on the floor of the cart, almost like goods.
Though it was humiliating, the children had no choice but to accept it. The cart took them through dusty roads, and the young Ambedkar realized how even a simple journey could turn into an ordeal for the so-called “untouchables.”
The experience left a strong impression on his mind. It was not just the distance they travelled but also the distance society created through caste discrimination that became unforgettable.

How does young Ambedkar experience caste discrimination at Masur railway station?
At Masur station, caste discrimination was very clear. When Ambedkar approached people for help, they first showed sympathy but withdrew as soon as they heard his caste. No one offered water, food, or a place to rest. They were treated as untouchables, as if their presence polluted others.
Even the stationmaster, who could have helped, refused to treat them with kindness. The children had to sit on the platform the whole day like abandoned passengers. Their thirst and hunger increased, but nobody dared to give them even a drop of water.
This cruel treatment showed Ambedkar, at a young age, the harsh realities of caste system—how it stripped people of humanity and compassion. He realized that untouchability was not just about social status but also about deep humiliation and denial of basic human needs.

Explain briefly how Ambedkar experienced ritual purity and caste hierarchy in his journey to Goregaon.
Ambedkar’s journey to Goregaon exposed him directly to the ideas of ritual purity and caste hierarchy. At Masur station, people refused to touch him or give him water because they believed it would make them impure. Their sense of caste purity was so strong that the suffering of children did not move them.
Later, even the cartman, though kinder than the others, forced the children to sit separately in his cart. This was another reminder that in society’s eyes they were “polluting.” The cartman was afraid that if they sat with him, he too would be considered impure by others.
Thus, throughout the journey, Ambedkar learned how caste rules governed social behavior in India. Even the simplest human actions—like drinking water, sitting in a cart, or asking for help—were controlled by the hierarchy of caste.

What lessons did Ambedkar learn from his experiences at Goregaon? Explain.
The painful journey to Goregaon became a turning point in young Ambedkar’s life. He learned that society was deeply unjust and that the caste system denied dignity and humanity to millions of people. The humiliation at Masur station and the cart journey taught him that the problem was not just personal but social.
From these bitter experiences, Ambedkar developed a strong sense of determination. He realized that education and courage were the only ways to fight against discrimination. These childhood lessons shaped his future mission to bring equality, justice, and self-respect to the oppressed communities of India.
Later in life, as a great leader, thinker, and the chief architect of the Indian Constitution, Ambedkar often recalled this journey. It gave him the fire to fight against untouchability and to build a society based on liberty, equality, and fraternity
