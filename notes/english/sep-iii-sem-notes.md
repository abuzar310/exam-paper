01. The Overcoat – Summary (Easy 10-Mark Answer) 
About the Author  
Nikolai Gogol (1809–1852) was a Russian writer known for his satirical and 
humorous style. His works often criticize social injustice and bureaucracy in 
Russia. 
 Summary 
The Overcoat is one of Nikolai Gogol’s most famous short stories. It tells the 
tragic and touching story of Akaky Akakievich, a poor government clerk in St. 
Petersburg. Through his life, Gogol highlights the problems of poverty, social 
injustice, and the cruelty of the bureaucratic system. 
 
Akaky Akakievich is a simple and innocent man who works as a copyist. He has 
no interest in anything except copying official documents. His life is very dull and 
lonely, and he is regularly mocked by his colleagues. He accepts all insults quietly 
without complaining, which shows his helplessness and gentle nature. He earns 
very little money and lives in extreme poverty. 
 
The main event of the story begins when Akaky’s old overcoat becomes 
completely worn out. The winter in St. Petersburg is harsh, and he desperately 
needs a warm coat. When the tailor Petrovich tells him that the coat cannot be 
repaired and he must get a new one, Akaky becomes worried because he cannot 
afford it. However, he decides to save money by cutting down his expenses. For 
months, he sacrifices small comforts such as food, candles, and tea. His 
determination shows how important the overcoat becomes in his life. 
 
When the new overcoat is finally made, it brings Akaky great joy. His colleagues 
admire it and even throw a small celebration in his honour. For the first time, he 
feels noticed and respected. But this happiness does not last long. On the night of 
the celebration, two thieves steal his overcoat.  
 
Akaky tries to seek help from the police and even approaches a high-ranking 
official, but no one helps him. The “Important Person” even scolds him harshly, 
which shocks Akaky deeply. 
 
The loss of the overcoat breaks his spirit. He falls sick and dies soon after. Later, 
his ghost appears on the streets, taking overcoats from others, including the 
Important Person. This supernatural ending symbolizes society’s guilt. 
 
Gogol uses Akaky’s tragic life to criticize the insensitivity of society and the 
corruption of officials. He shows that even a small act of kindness could save a 
life, while cruelty can destroy a person completely. The story reminds us of the 
importance of compassion and human dignity. 
 
Themes  
a) Poverty and Struggle, b) Social Injustice, c) Human Dignity, d) Bureaucracy 
and Power Abuse, e) Supernatural Element.

 II. Character Sketch of Akaky Akakievich Bashmachkin  
 
Akaky Akakievich is the central character of Nikolai Gogol’s The Overcoat. He is 
a poor government clerk who works as a copyist. His life is simple and 
monotonous. He enjoys copying documents and finds peace in his work. Akaky 
has no friends, no family, and no social life, which shows his lonely existence. 
 
He is extremely humble, shy, and gentle. He never argues with anyone, even when 
his colleague’s insult or mock him. Instead of reacting, he quietly continues his 
work. This shows his patience and innocent nature. He is also honest and 
hardworking, but his sincerity is not appreciated in the office. 
 
Akaky lives in poverty. His clothes are old, his room is bare, and he eats very 
little because he earns a very small salary. His old overcoat becomes a big 
problem in the cold winter. When he decides to buy a new one, it becomes the 
only excitement of his life. His dedication to saving every small coin for the 
overcoat shows his determination and sacrifice. 
 
The new overcoat brings Akaky rare moments of joy and respect. For the first 
time, his colleagues notice him and treat him kindly. But this happiness is short-
lived, as the overcoat is stolen, and Akaky receives no help from the officials. The 
loss leads to his emotional breakdown and death. 
 
Akaky’s character symbolizes the suffering of the poor and the cruelty of society. 
Through him, Gogol shows how an ordinary, harmless man can be destroyed by 
neglect and injustice. 
 
III. Character Sketch of Petrovich (the tailor)  
 
Petrovich is the tailor who stitche Akaky’s new overcoat. He is an important 
minor character in the story. He is described as a skilled but moody tailor. He 
often drinks, which affects his behaviour, and sometimes he argues with his 
customers. 
 
When Akaky brings his old overcoat for repair, Petrovich honestly explains that 
it cannot be fixed and a new one must be made. This shows that he is 
straightforward and practical. He charges a high amount for stitching the 
overcoat, which seems unfair, but it also reflects the economic condition of the 
time and the exploitation faced by the poor. 
 
Despite his rough behaviour, Petrovich completes the overcoat with great care. 
The new overcoat becomes a symbol of hope and happiness in Akaky’s life. 
Petrovich’s role is small but meaningful because he indirectly brings the only joy 
that Akaky experiences.

IV. Themes in The Overcoat  
 
Nikolai Gogol’s The Overcoat is not only a story about a poor clerk and his stolen 
coat; it is a powerful work rich in themes that criticise society, expose human 
suffering, and highlight the need for compassion. The story deals with several 
major themes such as poverty, social injustice, bureaucratic cruelty, human 
dignity, and the supernatural.  
 
One of the strongest themes in the story is poverty. Akaky Akakievich lives a life 
of extreme hardship. His salary is so small that even buying a new overcoat 
becomes a life-changing struggle. Gogol shows how poverty traps people and 
forces them to sacrifice their basic needs. Akaky’s months of saving and suffering 
for a simple coat represent the harsh reality of the lower class. 
 
Another important theme is social injustice and cruelty. Throughout the story, 
Akaky is ignored, insulted, and mistreated. His colleagues mock him for their 
entertainment, and no one supports him. Society sees him as insignificant 
because he is poor. Even when his overcoat is stolen, the authorities show no 
concern. This highlights how the poor are often denied justice and respect. 
 
The story also criticises the bureaucratic system. The “Important Person” 
represents the arrogance of those in power. Instead of helping Akaky, he scolds 
him and uses his position to show superiority. Gogol mocks the Russian 
bureaucracy by showing how officials care more about their status than the 
people they are supposed to serve. 
 
A key thematic idea in the story is human dignity. For Akaky, the overcoat is not 
just a piece of clothing; it is a symbol of hope, confidence, and respect. The way 
people treat him differently after he gets the new coat shows how society values 
appearance more than character. When the coat is taken away, Akaky loses all 
dignity and hope, which leads to his death. 
 
Finally, the supernatural element at the end adds a symbolic theme. Akaky’s 
ghost stealing overcoats shows the return of justice in a dark, ironic way. It 
suggests that the cruelty and neglect of society do not disappear—they come back 
to haunt the guilty. 
 
Through these themes, Gogol reveals the pain of ordinary people and teaches the 
importance of kindness, empathy, and humanity.

02. Kirubunkal – Long Answer (10 Marks) 
“Kirubunkal,” written by Dr. Ravikumar Neha, is a powerful story that 
combines reality, imagination, village gossip, and human psychology. The story 
exposes how rumours and false tales can destroy families and disturb the cultural 
harmony of a community. It also highlights how modern technology and 
changing values affect relationships. 
 
The story revolves around Seena (Sreenivasa), a careless young man who is 
addicted to mobile phones and reels. He compares his dark-skinned wife with the 
fair and attractive girls he watches online, which shows the influence of 
unrealistic digital images on the minds of today’s youth. Seena’s parents, Eeri 
and Mudli, represent hardworking rural people who struggle to maintain dignity 
in society. 
 
The turning point of the story is the mysterious death of Mudli, who is found 
dead near Chandranna’s farm. No one knows the truth, but villagers quickly 
invent their own versions of the story. Every person adds imagination and turns 
the incident into gossip. Some say Mudli insulted Chandranna’s family; some 
accuse him of an affair; others blame Chandranna for murder. These rumours 
show how society enjoys lies more than truth. 
 
An interesting and unique feature of the story is that the tales themselves become 
characters. They complain that human beings no longer create meaningful 
stories based on experience, but only spread lies, dirty jokes, and sensational 
rumours. The tales decide to take revenge by creating even more confusing 
stories that make society unstable. 
 
The Kirubunkal tree, an ancient and sacred tree, symbolizes the traditional 
culture of storytelling. It observes how human values are declining and warns 
that society is slowly losing kindness, unity, and truthfulness. 
Through these elements, “Kirubunkal” teaches that rumours can destroy lives, 
technology must be used responsibly, and traditional storytelling should be 
protected. The story encourages readers to think critically, to respect truth, and 
to preserve moral values in society. 
 
Themes in the story: 1. Theme of Rumour, Gossip, and False Tales,  
2. Dehumanisation in Modern Society,  3. Caste, Class, and Oppression,  
4. Corruption of Traditional Culture, 5. Symbolism of Kirubunkal,  
6. Breakdown of Moral and Social Values, 7. Psychological Decline of Seena. 
 
Conclusion: Kirubunkal is not just a story about a village—It is a critique of the 
entire modern world, where: truth is replaced by lies, culture is replaced by cheap 
entertainment, technology controls human minds, caste and power destroy justice, and 
old storytelling traditions are dying. Through powerful symbols and raw language, the 
story warns that unless society changes, both humans and their culture will continue to 
collapse.

II. Short Notes on for 05/10 marks  
 
1. Seena’s Obsession in Life 
 
Seena grows up as a lonely and confused young man after the mysterious death 
of his father, Mudli. Raised by his mother Eeri, he develops an unhealthy 
attachment to his mobile phone. Instead of involving himself in real life, 
relationships, or responsibilities, Seena spends all his time watching reels, videos 
of beautiful girls, and pornographic content. This makes him dissatisfied with his 
dark-skinned wife Shalini and even with himself. His obsession with fair skin and 
beauty leads him to constantly wash his face, hoping to become fair. The phone 
becomes his escape from reality, and he stops caring about family, marriage, or 
village life. He behaves like a child even after marriage, sharing intimate details 
of his life openly. Seena’s obsession symbolises how technology and fantasy 
distance young people from reality, pushing them into illusion and 
irresponsibility. 
 
2. Indian Tradition of Telling Tales  
 
The story highlights the rich Indian tradition of storytelling, where tales—called 
“kathe” or “stories”—become part of the community’s living culture. In the 
village, tales are not just entertainment; they shape beliefs, identity, and 
collective memory. Elders like Hirikateyajji carried these tales from one 
generation to another, giving life to nature, people, and culture. Tales travelled 
across villages, changing form over time, mixing truth and imagination. They 
explained strange events, moral issues, and social relations. However, in 
Kirubunkal, the sacred tradition weakens. Modern influences, false rumours, 
and sensational stories replace meaningful tales. Instead of wisdom, tales start 
spreading fear, lust, division, and lies. Through talking tales and mythical 
elements, the story shows how India’s storytelling heritage once guided society 
but is now threatened by misinformation and loss of values. 
 
3. Eeri’s Life After Mudli’s Death  
 
After Mudli’s mysterious and violent death, Eeri’s life becomes filled with 
hardship and loneliness. As a widow, she must raise Seena single-handedly. She 
deeply loves her son because he is the only memory of her dead husband. Eeri 
works hard to fulfil all of Seena’s wishes, even buying him a mobile phone, 
hoping it will make him happy. She hopes for government compensation after 
Mudli’s death, but it never arrives, adding to her worries. Her life becomes 
centered entirely around caring for Seena, who gradually becomes irresponsible 
and mentally unstable. Later, she arranges Seena’s marriage to Shalini, hoping 
marriage will bring stability, but Seena continues his unhealthy habits. Eeri 
silently suffers as village tales accuse her of immoral relationships, damaging her 
dignity. Her life represents the struggles of widows in rural India, who face 
poverty, gossip, and emotional burden without any support.

4. Attack on Mudli  
 
Mudli’s death forms the central mystery of Kirubunkal. On the dark night of the 
new moon, when he goes searching for his missing son Seena, he hears strange 
cries and moves towards Kirabunkal. Later that night, he is attacked by a group 
of men—followers of Sahukar Chandranna. They beat him brutally, leaving him 
speechless and half-dead. The reason for the attack is unclear, but many 
contradictory tales spread across the village: some claim Mudli had an affair 
with Chandranna’s wife, others say he misbehaved with Chandranna’s daughter 
Vimli, and some say Chandranna was involved with Eeri. These tales create 
multiple possibilities, showing how truth gets buried under rumours. Mudli 
collapses near Chandranna’s farm and dies. His death becomes the starting point 
for new tales, gossip, and fear in the village. The attack symbolises violence 
caused by caste politics, rumours, and power imbalance. 
 
5. Importance of Hirikateyajji  
 
Hirikateyajji is portrayed as the oldest and most respected elder among the 
“tales.” She represents wisdom, tradition, and the ancient storytelling culture of 
the village. While the other tales begin to spread sensational, immoral, and 
harmful stories, Hirikateyajji recognises the danger these new tales pose to 
society. She speaks with authority and concern, reminding the tales of their 
original duty—to guide humans, bring harmony, and create meaningful stories. 
Her warnings express the fear that the village is losing its moral compass. She 
also approaches Kirabunkal, the sacred origin of tales, seeking solutions to 
restore the village’s lost values. Through her character, the text conveys the 
message that elders and traditional knowledge are necessary to maintain balance 
in society. Hirikateyajji becomes a symbolic grandmother figure who tries to 
protect culture from being destroyed by modern distortions and irresponsible 
storytelling. 
 
6. Importance of Sahukar Chandranna  
 
Sahukar Chandranna is an influential and powerful figure in the village. His 
presence shapes many events in the story, especially the mysterious death of 
Mudli. Several tales revolve around him—some accuse him of having an affair 
with Eeri, others say Mudli was involved with his wife or daughter, and some 
tales even imply he killed Mudli to protect his family’s honour. Although the real 
truth is never known, Chandranna’s social status allows him to manipulate 
narratives and hide facts. 
 He uses the village’s fear during the sickness outbreak to claim Mudli died of 
disease, quickly burying him without proper rituals. This makes him a symbol of 
how people in power can control stories, spread misinformation, and influence 
public belief. Chandranna represents authority, fear, and the role of upper-class 
men in shaping the fate of weaker families like Eeri’s. His character exposes the 
injustice in rural power structures.

7. The Tales of the Village  
 
In Kirubunkal, tales are personified as living beings who move through the 
village, interact with each other, and influence human lives. Earlier, tales were 
meaningful, moral, and imaginative, giving life to the land and shaping cultural 
identity. They helped people understand their world. But over time, the tales 
change. They become sensational, vulgar, and harmful—focusing on rumours, 
sexual fantasies, and false accusations. They confuse truth and lies, causing fear, 
conflict, and misunderstanding among the villagers. The death of Mudli becomes 
a fertile ground for new tales to be born every day. These tales invade the lives of 
Mudli, Eeri, Chandranna, and Vimli, destroying their dignity. The text shows 
how stories can uplift society or corrupt it, depending on how they are used. The 
village becomes a factory of false storytelling, reflecting today's world of 
misinformation and viral content. 
 
8. Morality and the Village Tales  
 
The story highlights how village tales shape the community’s morality. Earlier, 
tales carried wisdom, ethical lessons, and respect for nature and people. They 
preserved cultural values. However, in the modern context of the story, morality 
collapses. Tales no longer teach good behaviour; instead, they spread lies, lust, 
and violence. They distort reality to entertain people, satisfying hidden desires 
rather than guiding them. False narratives lead to moral chaos in the village—
people judge Eeri, imagine sinful affairs, and gossip without concern for truth. 
Men fantasize about women mentioned in tales, and women fear for their safety 
and dignity. The tales show how morality weakens when a community loses 
empathy, responsibility, and truth. Through this shift, the author criticizes how 
modern society replaces genuine stories with corruption, fake news, and 
sensational gossip, leading to the decline of social values. 
 
9. The Village Life in ‘Kirubunkal’  
 
Village life in Kirubunkal is portrayed as a mix of tradition, poverty, 
superstition, and gossip. The village depends heavily on stories and rumours to 
understand events, especially mysterious ones like Mudli’s death. People’s lives 
are deeply influenced by caste hierarchy, economic inequality, and social 
expectations. Technology, like mobile phones, has entered village life but 
distorted values—for example, Seena becomes addicted to reels and adult 
content. The community is quick to suspect and judge others, creating false 
stories about relationships and scandals. Villagers live in fear during sickness 
outbreaks, showing their vulnerability. Nature also suffers; Kirabunkal, the life-
giving tree, weakens due to human neglect. The village survives through oral 
tales, celebrations, and old customs, but it is slowly collapsing under modern 
pressures and the spread of harmful gossip. The story reveals both the charm 
and the dark side of rural Indian life.

disconnected from the natural world. 
 
Thus, Kirubankal is significant because it represents the soul of the village, the 
centre of its stories, the guardian of its moral values, and the symbol of a 
harmonious relationship between humans and nature. Its decline reflects the 
decay of the entire community.

03. On the Way to Goregaon – Summary (10 Marks) 
 
“On the Way to Goregaon” is an autobiographical incident from Dr. B. R. 
Ambedkar’s childhood. It describes the painful experience he and his brothers 
faced due to caste discrimination during a journey. 
 
Ambedkar begins by introducing his family. His ancestors worked in the East 
India Company’s army, and his father was a Subhedar in the British Indian 
Army. His mother had passed away, so Ambedkar and his siblings were taken 
care of by their aunt in Satara. Although she was physically disabled, she lovingly 
looked after the children. 
 
During their summer vacation, Ambedkar’s father, who worked in Koregaon, 
asked the children to visit him. They were very excited because they had never 
travelled by train before. Their aunt prepared food and made new clothes for 
them. A tonga took them to the railway station, and they boarded the train to 
Masur. 
 
However, when they reached Masur, their difficulties began. No one came to 
receive them. The station master initially behaved kindly, thinking they were 
Brahmin children. But the moment Ambedkar revealed that they were Mahars 
(considered untouchables), the station master’s behaviour changed completely. 
He walked away and refused to help them. 
 
The children tried to hire a bullock cart to go to Koregaon. But every cartman 
refused to carry them because untouchables were not allowed to sit in or touch 
upper-caste carts. Finally, one cartman agreed but on the condition that the boys 
themselves would drive the cart while he walked beside it. After some time, the 
cartman left to have food and later returned. He sat in the cart due to fear of the 
dark but still kept distance from the children. The boys were frightened and 
cried, suspecting he might harm them. 
 
Late at night, they reached the toll-collector’s hut. Even there, they were not 
allowed to drink water because of caste rules. Hungry, thirsty, and tired, they had 
to wait until their father finally arrived and took them home. 
 
This whole journey deeply affected young Ambedkar. He realised how cruel and 
inhuman the practice of untouchability was. The experience shaped his 
determination to fight against caste discrimination and work for equality and 
justice throughout his life.

II. Short Notes  
 1. Ambedkar’s Father 
Ambedkar’s father was a disciplined, responsible, and principled man who 
served in the Army of the East India Company. He rose to the respectable rank of 
Subhedar, which shows his dedication and leadership qualities. After retirement, 
he worked as a cashier at Koregaon, supervising the government’s famine-relief 
work. Although he could not visit his children often due to his duties, he 
maintained strong communication with them. He carefully planned their journey 
to Koregaon and instructed them about the travel details, showing his care and 
concern as a parent. His values of honesty, hard work, and discipline had a deep 
influence on young Ambedkar. Even though he was physically absent during the 
incident, his presence is strongly felt through the children’s efforts to reach him. 
Ambedkar remembered his father as a person who balanced duty and affection, 
and his disciplined background shaped Ambedkar’s own personality and later 
commitment to social reform. 
 
 2. Ambedkar’s Aunt  
Ambedkar’s aunt played a significant role in his childhood, especially after the 
death of his mother. She was extremely loving and gentle, but physically weak. 
She was a dwarf and had trouble walking, which made daily tasks very difficult 
for her. Despite her limitations, she took care of Ambedkar and his brothers with 
great affection. Since she could not cook properly, the children had to manage 
their own meals and often prepared simple dishes like pulav. Before their 
journey, she lovingly arranged food for them with the help of neighbours. Her 
emotional reaction when the children left for Koregaon shows her deep 
attachment to them. Although she could not offer physical help, her presence 
gave the children emotional support and comfort. Ambedkar remembered her 
kindness throughout his life. Her character represents the silent, caring women in 
families who support children with love, even when they cannot contribute 
physically. She added warmth and humanity to Ambedkar’s early life. 
 
 3. The Station Master at Masur  
The station master at Masur is an important character who reflects the caste 
attitudes of society. When Ambedkar and his brothers arrived at the station, the 
station master treated them kindly, assuming they were Brahmins because of 
their clean clothes and polite behaviour. However, when Ambedkar innocently 
revealed that they were Mahars (untouchables), the station master’s expression 
changed immediately. He withdrew from them and hesitated to help further, 
showing the deep-rooted social prejudice of the time. Yet, when he noticed that 
the children were helpless and stranded, he felt sympathy and tried to arrange a 
bullock cart for them. However, even his authority could not convince the 
cartmen because none of them wanted to carry untouchable children. Finally, he 
came up with a solution where the children would drive the cart themselves. His 
mixed behaviour—kindness followed by discrimination—clearly shows how even 
well-placed people were victims of caste-based thinking. He represents society’s 
inability to rise above untouchability.

 4. The Cart Driver  
 
The cart driver symbolizes both the cruelty and helplessness created by caste 
rules. At first, he refused to take Ambedkar and his brothers because carrying 
untouchables would “pollute” him. He only agreed when he learned that the 
children themselves would drive the cart, allowing him to walk beside it. This 
shows how deeply caste restrictions controlled everyday life. During the journey, 
however, his behaviour changed. When darkness surrounded them, he jumped 
into the cart out of fear, forgetting his earlier concerns about pollution. This 
frightened the children, who suspected that he might harm them for their gold 
ornaments. Still, he continued guiding them and finally brought them to the toll-
collector’s hut at midnight. The cart driver also advised Ambedkar to lie about 
his caste to get water, which shows that he understood the harshness of social 
rules but still followed them. His divided behaviour reflects the contradictions of 
caste society—fear of untouchability mixed with human concern. 
 
 5. Water Issues and Ambedkar  
 
Water plays a symbolic and practical role in Ambedkar’s journey. Throughout 
their travel, Ambedkar and his brothers struggled to get clean drinking water 
simply because they were untouchables. At the riverbed, the only available water 
was filthy, mixed with mud and cattle waste, and unfit for drinking. Later, at the 
toll-collector’s hut, Ambedkar tried to get water by pretending to be a Muslim, 
speaking in Urdu to sound convincing. But even then, the toll collector refused to 
help, showing how deeply prejudice controlled basic human needs. The children 
were forced to sleep hungry, despite having plenty of food, because they could not 
drink water. This helplessness made a strong impact on young Ambedkar’s mind. 
He realised that untouchability was not just social humiliation—it affected basic 
survival. The denial of water became a powerful symbol of social oppression, and 
this painful memory stayed with Ambedkar throughout his life, inspiring him to 
fight against caste injustice. 
 
 6. Ambedkar and His Brothers  
Ambedkar and his brothers began their journey with excitement. It was their 
first train journey, and they wore new clothes, carried food, and even bought 
lemonade at the station. However, their joy soon turned into fear and suffering. 
At Masur station, no one came to receive them, and after learning their caste, the 
station master and cartmen refused to help. The children had to drive the cart 
themselves, travelled through darkness, and faced loneliness and hunger. Their 
fear increased when the cart driver suddenly sat beside them in the dark. They 
even suspected that he might harm them for their ornaments. At the toll hut, they 
were denied water again because of untouchability. Despite all these hardships, 
the children remained united and courageous. The incident taught them the 
harsh reality of caste discrimination at a young age. This painful journey shaped 
Ambedkar’s understanding of social injustice and influenced his future fight for 
equality.

 7. Social Injustice and Ambedkar – 05/10 Marks  
 
The essay clearly exposes the cruelty of social injustice faced by Ambedkar and 
other untouchables. Even as a nine-year-old boy, Ambedkar faced humiliation at 
every step—at the railway station, on the road, and even when asking for water.

 8. Ambedkar’s Train Journey – 05/10 Marks  
 
Ambedkar’s first train journey began with great joy and excitement. He and his 
brothers wore new clothes, carried delicious food prepared by neighbours, and 
even enjoyed lemonade at the station. They eagerly boarded the train and looked 
forward to meeting their father. However, the real hardships began when they 
reached Masur. No one arrived to receive them, and after revealing their caste, 
the station master and cart drivers turned away from them. The children had to 
drive the cart themselves, travelled alone at night, and faced hunger and fear. 
The journey turned into a painful experience of caste discrimination.  
Despite the excitement of the train ride, the incident taught Ambedkar a lifelong 
lesson: untouchability followed people everywhere, even during joyful moments. 
The journey became a turning point in his life, awakening his awareness of social 
injustice. What started as a happy experience ended as a powerful reminder of 
the cruelty of caste-based discrimination. 
 
 III. What lessons did Ambedkar learn from his experiences at Goregaon? 
Explain.  (10 marks – 250 to 300 words) 
 
Dr. B. R. Ambedkar’s journey to Goregaon as a young boy became one of the 
most important lessons of his life. The humiliating experiences he faced due to 
caste discrimination opened his eyes to the harsh realities of society. 
 
First, Ambedkar realised how deeply the idea of untouchability was rooted in the 
minds of people. At Masur station, the station master spoke kindly to him at first, 
thinking he was from an upper caste. But the moment Ambedkar mentioned that 
they were Mahars, the station master’s attitude changed. This taught Ambedkar 
that caste could immediately decide how a person was treated in society.

Second, he learned that untouchability denied even basic human needs. The 
cartmen refused to take him and his brothers in their carts. They were not 
allowed to sit, touch, or even pay for the service. Later, at the toll-collector’s hut, 
they were not given even a drop of water. From this, Ambedkar understood that 
the caste system controlled every small aspect of life—food, water, travel, and 
social contact. 
 
Third, he realised how people blindly believed in the idea of “ritual purity.” The 
upper castes behaved as if touching an untouchable would pollute them. 
Ambedkar saw that this belief made people cruel, heartless, and unjust. He 
understood that such practices were not religious truths but social evils created 
by ignorance. 
 
Finally, this painful journey taught Ambedkar the value of dignity and equality. 
Even though he was a child, he was treated as inferior. This experience made him 
determined to fight against caste discrimination. It sowed the seeds of his future 
mission—to create a society where all human beings are treated with respect, 
regardless of caste. 
 
 In conclusion, Ambedkar learned that caste discrimination was deeply unfair 
and that it destroyed human values. His experience at Goregaon strengthened his 
resolve to dedicate his life to justice, equality, and the upliftment of the
